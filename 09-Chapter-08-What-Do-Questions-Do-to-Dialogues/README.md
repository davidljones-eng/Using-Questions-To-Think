## The big idea

Questioning is **something we do with others**, never in isolation. When we question ourselves, that "other" is the otherness within us (Ricoeur). **Dialogue is the sustained movement of question-and-answer complexes** that pass through dialectical initiatives, furtherings, and completions.

Questioning does the same transforming work at each of the book's three levels:

```
sentence perceived       ──questioning──▶  complete thought understood
exposition perceived     ──questioning──▶  reasoning understood
conversation perceived   ──questioning──▶  DIALOGUE understood
```

A **conversation** is a continuous stretch of perceived speech. A **dialogue** is an understanding of the dialectical dynamism among the complete thoughts, reasonings, and questionings in it. Because dialogue is something we do with others, **questioning has to be rooted in listening with others.**

The chapter makes four moves:

1. **Dialogue is not debate.**
2. **Genuine questioning is a form of listening**: "a way we listen with our mouths."
3. **Shared questions** make it possible to understand what others say.
4. **Reaching an understanding** is defined precisely as a **fusion of horizons**.

## 1. Dialectical dialogue is not debate (pp. 162–167)

### Debate culture

- Many schools have a debate club. **None has a dialogue team, and there are no dialogue competitions.** Who benefits from training us in debate instead of dialogue?
- Debate teams have tried to win by any means necessary, and the debate format itself has been shown to be **complicit with white male privilege** (Kraft 2014).
- The same thing happens in national politics, for example Ronald Reagan's **"debategate"** scandal (Kondracke 1983).
- **Etymology:** *-bat* means fight or battle, and *de-* intensifies it ("completely"). A debate is **"a battle to the bitter end."** It ends with a judge's decision.
- **Propositionalism and debate fit together.** Both are about **defending a conclusion, conclusively.**

### How debate polices intelligibility

- Debate **imposes conditions of intelligibility** that other communities don't necessarily share.
- Debaters say **"You cannot say that,"** and they **use fallacy accusations to police speech**. In doing so they unconsciously make logic serve their political and economic interests.
- **Exclusivist monotheism** example: "Only one religion can be true," and the speaker always assumes it's their own. Dickman imagines the reverse: *"Only one religion can be true, and it is Daoism. I happen to be a Reformed Christian, though."* This shows **reason subordinated to self-interest**.

### What dialogue and debate share, and where they part

- Both **presuppose common ground**. Disagreement can't be specific without it.
- **Debate and apologetics** start from **pre-established positions** and use reason **instrumentally** to defend and persuade. They **produce no new understanding** for the debater.
- **Extreme defensive debate** doesn't even support its conclusion. It argues only that **"at least we are not irrational"** to believe it (Plantinga & Wolterstorff 1991). This:
  - shifts the topic to the *conditions of intelligibility*
  - sets its own boundaries of rationality and **threatens** others with the label "irrational"
  - demands that others play by rules **"they seem to be making up as they go"**
- **Gadamer:** "In our understanding, which we imagine is so innocent… the other presents itself so much in terms of our own selves that there is no longer a question of self and other."
- If the order of intelligibility itself is in question, demonstrating that you're "not irrational" accomplishes nothing. It just lets you **rest complacently in exclusivism.**
- **"Talk *to* you later"**: does "to" mean *at* or *with*? Those defending their non-irrationality are **speaking *at* others, not *with* them.**

| **Debate** | **Dialogue** |
|---|---|
| Stops at a **concluding thesis** | Reaches understandings that **touch off further dialogues** |
| A conclusion is **the end** | Conclusions serve **multiple roles**, e.g., as premises for further arguments |
| A stop is a **conclusion** | A stop is an **interruption** (fatigue, hunger, running out of things to say for now) |
| Ends historical movement | **Preserves our historical dynamism** |

> **Gadamer:** "Every dialogue also has an **inner infinity** and no end. One breaks it off… But every such break has an intrinsic relation to the **resumption** of the dialogue."

Why do good dialogues end? **Do they really end?** Genuine questioning makes further dialogue possible.

### Not the inverse of debate either

- Genuine questioning isn't simply **letting yourself be persuaded and absorbed** by others. Subordinating yourself to their worldview is **no better than complacent exclusivism.**
- Dickman rejects the **Levinasian "ethics of the hostage"** here, the idea that letting others impose on us is the alternative.
- We couldn't do it anyway: **"Every time we try to walk a mile in another's shoes, we bring our own shoes with us."**
- What genuine questions show instead is a **readiness to follow the subject matter wherever the winds of dialogue blow** (Socrates). Dialogues and the understandings they reach are **unpredictable**. *Subject matters radiate predicative possibilities, and topics radiate reasoning possibilities.*
- Questioning in dialogue assumes we **know our limits** and don't know ahead of time what will come of engaging with diversity.

### Standpoints move

- We can't think everything at once, so we **draw it out bit by bit** (Gadamer). Dialogue starts from a standpoint, but **responsible dialogue, the kind that engages instead of evading, doesn't require imposing conditions of intelligibility.**
- **Our standpoints keep changing**, even when we mistake them for a stable essence. Some ways to shift:
  - question as a **student of political science**, not as a representative of a party
  - take the **child's** perspective, not the parent's
  - move from **psychological** questions to **sociological** ones
- **Ricoeur:** we always perceive "from somewhere… in a movement of **gradual transfer**," and standpoints are far from fixed.
- **Levinas:** "**proximity is a difference**." It "empties me of all consistency."
- **If someone who calls themselves a Republican questions genuinely, their label tells us nothing about where the dialogue will go.**

### Dialogue is fragile: conversation stoppers

- "*There is a time for talk and a time for action. Now is the time for action.*" Dickman asks: **why is it always time for action?**

**Bias**

- A bias isn't just having a viewpoint. Having one is necessary for bias but not sufficient.
- **Definition:** *an unthinking habit of disposition toward a position, involving a conscious or unconscious refusal to consider reasonable or dialogically available alternatives, and often resulting in unfair actions.*
- Biases are **cognitive shortcuts**. They're sometimes useful, since we can't think everything through, but they **shape how we see the world, others, and ourselves** (DiCarlo 2011). They cause harm when misapplied, as with **stereotypes** and **oppressive discrimination**, and they color even our thinking about thinking.

| Source of bias | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Historical situation and finitude** | Prejudices as effects of history. **Wanting to eradicate all prejudice is itself a prejudice against prejudice.** The goal is to **understand prejudices and put them at risk**, not escape them |
| **Biological** | Predispositions, brain chemistry, health or illness, emotions |
| **Cultural** | Family background, ethnicity, in-group/out-group identities, peer pressure, media |

**Bad faith (Sartre)**, the worst conversation stopper

- **Bad faith** means saying what you **cannot really mean**. For Sartre, it's using your freedom to argue that you have no freedom, or living as though you had none.
  - *Example:* students who **voluntarily** enroll in a course and then say the professor is **"making"** them do an assignment.
- Everyday self-referentially incoherent bad faith:
  - "**No one knows what they're talking about.**"
  - "**That's just your opinion.**"
  - "**I couldn't help it.**"
- **Bad faith isn't lying.** A liar still cares about the truth. Bad faith is **bullshit** (Frankfurt), indifferent to whether anything is true or false.

### The fundamental conversation stopper: propositionalist ideology

- **Ideology**, defined: *an unconscious, comprehensive distribution of material and symbolic power, plus a worldview that props up that distribution, governed by hierarchically ordered binary notions, serving the material and symbolic interests of dominant economic and cultural classes* (Baggini 2010).

| **Worldview** | **Ideology** |
|---|---|
| **Conscious** conceptual framework within which knowledge is built | **Unconscious** |
| Has explicit spokespersons (self-identified Buddhists, Republicans) | An **ideologue** serves an institution's interests **without recognizing it** |
| — | *Example:* US mail doesn't run on Sundays, a Christian inheritance. **An atheist citizen still serves Christian interests unintentionally** |
| — | The ideologue doesn't just fail to recognize their ideology. **They *misrecognize* it** (Bell 2009). That's why it's insidious |

> **It serves ideologies for us to believe that "ideology" just means "worldview."**

- **Can propositionalist ideologues be persuaded by argument? No.** They rest exclusively on their own idea of what counts as rational persuasion, and the ideology has **already co-opted the conditions of "argumentation."**
- So **critique of ideology can't argue with it directly.** It has to **expose its tactics for preserving domination**, for the sake of **emancipating the marginalized** (Freire). Related strategies:
  - **Kierkegaard:** *indirect communication*
  - **Habermas:** treat society like a **psychoanalytic patient**, with a **hermeneutic of suspicion**
- **Dickman's strategy is genuine questioning.** We can't eradicate prejudice and ideology wholesale. We're finite and historical. **Each bias overcome gives way to new ones, and each conclusion becomes a premise** (the *sediment* side of tradition). But **questioning puts biases, prejudices, and ideologies at risk** of critique, response, transformation, and fusion of horizons. **Questioning is the opening for the *innovative* side of tradition, and that opening corresponds to our ability to listen.**

## 2. Questions are a way of listening with others (pp. 167–174)

### Correcting earlier theories

- Erotetic logic and earlier phenomenologies **start from judgments** and derive questions from them. They privilege answers, which makes questioning look **parasitic**. They mention dialogue only in passing.
- **Husserl** brackets out dialogue and treats questions as the solitary ego's **frustration** over uncertainty, seeking a decisive judgment. For him a question merely expresses a subjective feeling.
- **Dickman's correction:** genuine questioning isn't like **"ouch,"** which just expresses my pain. Questioning is "**tied intrinsically to its being addressed to some other person**" (Schumann & Smith). **Just as reading is always reading *to* another, questioning is done *with* another.**

### The problem of otherness

- Twentieth-century crises of nihilism have given way to "a more fundamental problem… **the situation of confronting otherness**" (Klemm 1987). The otherness of others exceeds **instrumental reasoning** and the technocratic **logic of equivalence**.
- **Disenfranchised voices** are often left with nothing but protest:
  - **COVID-19 relief (mid-2020):** support went to citizens and documented immigrants, but not to **undocumented immigrants** who make up much of the essential agricultural workforce. Lowering their wages was even proposed.
  - **Professional athletes** protesting racist police brutality peacefully and with good information were **harassed by the Trump administration and its supporters**.
  - **Even with protests, have people been heard?**
- **The task (Klemm):** "uncover what is questionable and what is genuine in the self and the other, while **opening the self to the other and allowing the other to remain other**."

### Listening isn't about ears

- Dickman isn't privileging hearing over the other senses. **Listening is the dialectical counterpart of *speech*, not of sound.** A Deaf person signing can ask genuine questions and reach understanding. **We hear with our whole body** (Ihde 2007).
- **Genuine questioning is receptive, not assertive.** Questions don't *say* anything. **Ihde:** "it is to the **invisible** that listening may attend."
- **The auditory field:**
  - It is an opening onto the networks of significance that make up our worlds.
  - It is **omnidirectional** (we're surrounded by sound), while vision is **unidirectional**.
  - We can shift focus without moving our heads.
  - Even in an **anechoic chamber** we hear "my breathing, the 'whine' of my nervous system… the bloodstream."
  - **Meaningful sound appears as *voiced*.** It speaks. *The teakettle's whistle calls to us.*
- **Spoken discourse is only the center of significant sound**, inside a wider horizon of the **unsaid**. To listen with understanding is to **enter a wider situation of significance**.
- **Gadamer:** "Hearing is an **avenue to the whole** because it is able to listen to the *logos*."
- **Listening is the opposite of speech, not of deafness.** As speech's dialectical companion, it's the **avenue to reaching an understanding**.

### Spoken, signed, and written discourse

- When we speak or sign, **unspoken context comes along**: noises, gestures, facial expressions. A word written alone on a page makes **the reader supply the context**, so writers have to add more words.
- **Speaking clings to writing.** Beginning readers "sound out" words. As fluency grows, we read silently.
- **Spoken, signed, or written, all discourse needs understanding's receptivity.** We don't make statements "in ontological outer space."
- **Listening is foundational to dialogue**, a condition for deciding what the other says. It's **the fundamental openness that defines human being.**
  - **Irigaray:** preserve "an opening… to listen to the other as other, as the one whom we cannot appropriate."
  - **Gadamer:** "Belonging together always also means being able to listen to one another."

### Listening is active

| Feature of skilled listening | Source |
|---|---|
| **Concentration and interpretation** | — |
| **"Evacuating"** areas of the dialogical field so others can take them up. Listening as "**contraction**, stepping back and creating a void into which the other may enter" | Lipari 2004 |
| **Suspending prejudices**, relative **detachment from one's own needs and interests** | Beatty 1999 |
| Suspending your claim to accurate categorization and treating your interpretations as **revisable** | — |
| "Openness… involves recognizing that I myself must **accept some things that are against me**, even though no one else forces me" | Gadamer |
| Suspending **reactions** as well, e.g., **anger** at what's said | — |
| "**Renunciation of a predominantly moulding and ordering activity**" in hope of a new quality of relationship | Fiumara 1990 |
| Listening in **good faith**: others recognize their own meaning in our interpretations. Keeping our **"inner voice"** from intruding | — |
| "A listening environment is **not improvised**… the product of a **strenuous** process of conception, growth, and devoted attention" | Fiumara 1990 |
| "A mode of being which is **not yet a mode of doing** and which avoids the alternative of **subjection and revolt**" | Ricoeur 1974 |
| **"Obedience"** comes from Latin ***ob-audire***, "a listening 'from below'" | Ihde 2007 |

### Listening is spoken

- Listeners are rarely silent. **They speak their listening.**
- **Response tokens** (Gardner 2001):

| Token | Function |
|---|---|
| "**mmm…**" | encourage the speaker to continue |
| "**oh!**" | acknowledge or take note |
| "**okay**" | signal readiness for a topic change |

- **Response tokens ≠ contributions.** Contributions (agreement, criticism) take a **position**. Tokens only show we're **taking note** (Bublitz 1988). **Taking note is necessary for taking a position, but not the other way around.**
- **Silence doesn't mean listening.** On a phone call when the other person goes quiet, you wonder: *are they even still there?* We have to **display** listening, in words as well as body language and eye contact.

> **Listening isn't just a faculty of sensation like hearing. It's a *mode of speaking*.**

### Genuine questions: listening with our mouths

- We ask genuine questions to make sure we hear what's said, to take note of it and understand its meaning.
- We **build tentative interpretations and test them**: "**Is this what you mean?**"
- **"Taking note" means understanding**, which means actually asking the question the sentence answers. Recognizing that noise is happening isn't enough.
- **Deficit-driven questions** don't embody listening. They're demands that **assert control** and require "*the*" answer.
- **Surplus-driven genuine questions** come from a **passion for more**. They **occur to us** when we're caught up in the play of dialogue. They're the **"unfurling radiance of a subject matter"** that invites further dialogue. We ask them "with **invitational enthusiasm** out of a surplus of love for more life."

> **"In receiving a genuine question from another, we are hearing ourselves being heard."**

### Hospitable to answerers, not just answers

- Genuine questions show **respect for others** and for **their authority over their own speech**. They show we're **listening, not telling others what they're saying**, and that we have no ulterior aim.
- We **hesitate** because we're unsure we heard correctly, so we **ask** instead of imposing our own constructions.
- **Our questions concern what the other says, not the other as such.** "*Oh, I get you*" is a category mistake, since the other person isn't a complete thought.
  - Understanding isn't **getting inside others and reliving their experiences**, which was the aim of **Romanticist and modernist hermeneutics**.
  - **Empathy ≠ understanding.** We can understand without empathizing and empathize without understanding.
  - **Gadamer:** we understand "**not the particular individual but what he says**." Therapy and criminal interrogation focus on the individual and so aren't genuine attempts to reach understanding.
- Responsive questions **neither assimilate otherness nor impose hegemonic intelligibility.**
- **Levinas:** the other "does not belong to the intelligible sphere to be explored. He **stands in proximity**."
- **Why speak at all instead of thinking privately?** Saying what we think is an **excess**, a "surplus" emission (Levinas). Questioning **addresses and invokes** others instead of reducing them to representations. **We can't control what others do with what we say**, which is why genuine questions **can't be reduced to the imperative**: they stay open "**without the power to coerce or command**."
- If we try to understand *them* instead of *what they say*, we turn them into a topic or object. Respect means treating them as **equally legitimate contributors**.

### Proximity is established by the question

- Genuine questions are how we **suspend judgment and contract ourselves to receive** what is said. We **expose ourselves** and stand near the other **responsively, not reactively**. Only that **vulnerability** lets what they say "**enter**" our dehiscent body, our world, our field of significance.
- **The telephone:** we answer the ring (the "call") with a question, "**Hello?**" Answering services open with a statement, "**Hello.**" That convention tells callers whether they've reached a person or a recording. **The question, not bodily contact, establishes proximity.**
- The otherness of the other is "**otherwise than understandable**," and that's fine. Understanding is limited to complete thoughts, no more and no less.

## 3. Share questions to understand what others say (pp. 174–181)

### Do we "put" questions?

- Genuine questioning is **intrinsically intersubjective**. It's a mode of **speaking**, and all speaking is *with* others, or *at* them.
- **Heidegger:** "The authentic attitude of thinking cannot be the putting of questions… but to **listen to that which our questioning vouchsafes**."
- Dickman's reply: **genuine questions aren't "put."** They **occur, arise, present themselves, strike us**. They're as much a **passion as an action**. We do "put" **calculative questions and typical interrogatives**, the ones reducible to commands.

### Understanding a question means asking it

- Questions **don't "mean" anything**. Answers do, as complete thoughts.
- We can **consider** a judgment without **meaning** it. Appropriating it takes more.
- **The only way to understand a question, to "mean" it, is to ask it.**
  - **Gadamer:** "There can be no tentative or potential attitude of questioning… Even when a person says such and such question might arise, this is already a real questioning that simply masks itself, out of either caution or politeness."
- We can decide not to say a question out loud, but **we can't decide whether it occurs to us.** For a question to be what it is, it has to be **adopted**.
- So **genuine questioning is closer to passivity than to intentional activity.** Its *occurrent* character is what makes it questioning.

### Vulnerability and love

- **Invitational questions are the speech counterpart of the vulnerability of our ears.**
- **This is a form of love.** **Kierkegaard:** people who love others do something to themselves: **they make room for others.** Genuine questions make room for **the contributions of others and for the subject matter itself**. *Opening spaces for genuine dialogue is an act of love.*
- In listening, **we risk our selfhood** by taking others' questions and answers seriously as possible disclosures of truth.
  - **Levinas:** "the risky uncovering of oneself, in sincerity, the breaking up of inwardness and the abandon of all shelter, exposure to traumas, vulnerability."
- Speaking risks being misunderstood. **Questioning as active listening is an even greater risk.**

### Responsive, not reactive

- You can't merely *react* to a genuine question. **It has to be asked before it can be answered.**
- *Example:* "**Are 'good books' also 'good reads'?**" Do you answer reflexively? Or do you repeat the question with a "**huh**" to make room for responding?
- The world does make reactive demands: a child who has to go to the hospital, catastrophic weather, political forces. Those can't be negotiated. **But in language, reaction can be sublimated into responsivity.**
- **A genuine question is a hesitation** that helps us **resist projecting prejudices onto experience** (Al-Saji 2014).
- Many questions, like listening well, are **ecstatic events** in which we're caught up in something bigger than our intentions.

### Their question becomes *our* question

> Because genuine questioning is more passion than deliberate action, **when we hear another ask a question we must ask it too in order to understand it. Their question becomes our question. We *share* in genuine questioning.**

- **Hermeneutic priority explains both *understanding* meanings and *transferring* them.** To understand others, they don't just answer *my* questions. **I have to ask *theirs*.**
- What they say may not answer anything I'm asking. It may answer questions **they're** already asking. *Can I position myself to ask their question?* **If not, there's no dialogue.**
- **Answers often divide us. Shared questions relate us**, in ways deeper than assimilative empathy.
- **Contact in questioning isn't absorption.** It doesn't cancel the other's alterity or suppress mine. It's a **difference-preserving relationship**, and that is what lets me **consider her answers as meanings**.
- **Levinas, again:** the request and prayer inside a question attest to a **relation to the other person**, the question's *originary* modality. In a **DRIP** culture, if information were all we needed, **we could just read nametags**.

> **"Questioning is not about getting the answers, but embodying relationship with others."**

### Whose question is it?

- When we both ask it, it's **ours**. You may have started it, but now it's **shared**.
- **Who benefits from "possessing" a question ("*my* question is…") instead of being possessed by shared questioning?**

**Classroom example: Aristotle on friendship**

- Students struggle with Aristotle's theory when they **don't share his question**: *What is the most fulfilling or complete kind of friendship?*
- **That question isn't really Aristotle's.** He didn't invent friendship.
- Traditional college students, moving from high school into adult life, are **well positioned to ask it**. They wonder who their true friends are.
- When students **join their questions to Aristotle's**, they can **consider his theory as a possible answer**, understand his conclusions as meanings, and **evaluate how well the theory answers the question**.

### Shared questioning is ethical

- **Žižek's authoritarian:** "*It is I who will ask the questions here!*" That fits **epistemic imperatives**.
- With genuine questioning, **the questioners' positions are indeterminate**, so it involves no deliberate domination.
- Only answers count as meanings to understand, so **the other is respected as a co-explorer**, not assimilated.
- It's not a privative need to subordinate others. It's **a desire for a surplus of possible meanings through shared exploration**.

### The real subject of dialogue

> The indeterminacy of "us" keeps us oriented toward the real subject of conversation: **neither you, nor me, nor even us, but the subject matter.**

**Can we be sure we're asking the "same" question?**

- Western individualism and Cartesian subjectivity lead to worries like "**How do I know your 'red' isn't my 'green'?**" Suspend that natural attitude.
- **Sharing a question means sharing a language**: shared conventions for identifying subjects and framing them with predicates, or proficiency in translating between languages.
- Subject matters stand out because **cultural traditions hand them down**. Inheriting a language means taking on **fields of predicative intelligibility**, the language games of a ***Lebensform*** (form of life).

**"What year is it?"**

- "**2021 CE**" brings in secularized Christian coordinates. For Muslims it's "**1442 AH**."
- **Asked as a genuine question** and not a request for information, it leads us to reflect on:
  - **what time itself is** (the subject)
  - **the politics of era-dating systems** (the possible predicates)
  - **how languages relate to truths**: both dates are true, yet they can't seem to be true in the same way at once, and asking which is "truly true" seems absurd

> **The point of a genuine question isn't so much for the other to *answer* it as for the other to *ask it with us*.**
> **Shared questions make a statement a *response within a dialogue* instead of a random expression "coming out of nowhere and receding back into nowhere." Questions transfer meaning from one person to another.**

**Questions for sharing others' questions:**

- What subject matter does the other have something to say about?
- How do they apply predicates to it?
- Do we have a shared language to think together in?
- Is what they say inferentially related to other statements?
- **If we don't share their questions, their answer, and indeed their very voice, is lost on us.**

### Babel and translation

- **The myth of Babel:** we're dispersed across language games, and our values can seem **incommensurable** (MacIntyre 1988).
- Think of cultural diversity and individual difference as **analogous to, and intertwined with, linguistic diversity**. Then sharing questions takes the **hard labor of learning new languages and language games**, which is to say **translation**.
- **Ricoeur:** "There is no universal language; nevertheless anyone seems capable of learning an additional language… **We have always translated**… is it not with something similar to this **linguistic hospitality** that we achieve a gradual understanding of… beliefs we call foreign?"
- **Claiming untranslatability reflects a false self-sufficiency** that grounds **hegemonic ethnocentrism**: we assume our culture is complete and superior.
- Like a mother tongue, inherited culture **constrains our starting point but makes it possible to build bridges, not barriers**.
- This lateral movement of understanding proceeds **aoristically**, perhaps to the point where earlier commitments lose their hold. **In sharing questions, we yield our horizons to the importance of what the other asks.**
- **Levinas:** if one is **deaf to the petition in questioning**, everything gets oriented toward an exclusive truth, and one stays inside that ontology's design.
- **The more language games we learn, the more fluent we become and the more fluid possibilities stay.** Like being absorbed in a game, genuine questioning **draws us in and fills us with the animating spirit of the subject**.

### Considering ≠ adopting

- Genuine questioning **makes it possible to consider meanings. It doesn't force us to mean any of them.**
- Considering *which* year it is, or *whose* year, doesn't commit us to either dating system.
- **Taking up one option as *the* answer means taking responsibility** for a determination within the open predicative possibilities.

> **In shared questioning we're already *co-responsible* on the way to fusing horizons.**

## 4. A phenomenology of integrating questions (pp. 181–189)

### Reaching understanding, defined precisely

- Dialogue's aim (Ch. 7) is **being at one on a subject matter**, which means reaching agreement or understanding. Now it can be stated more precisely.
- **Shared questioning makes it possible to transfer, and so share, meanings.** In a genuine question I don't so much intend to *know* something as intend to **hear what someone else has to say**, "in such a way that something **new and unpredictable** emerges for the both of us."

**Tillich on why Christian evangelism in Asia failed:**

> "The difficulty with the highly developed religions of Asia… is not so much that they reject the Christian answer as answer, as that… **they do not ask the question to which the Gospel gives the answer**. To them the Christian answer is no answer because they have not asked the question."

- Dickman doesn't quote this to help evangelists. **It isn't about forcing others to ask our questions.** Refusing to ask another's question, or to learn their language, **may be in one's interest**, especially when those others are complicit with **imperialism and colonialism**.
- **The point:** an answer makes no sense apart from the question it addresses, and **reaching understanding requires a shared language.**

### Fusion of horizons

- **Gadamer:** a fusion of horizons happens when partners **reach some agreement about a subject**. If we can't even agree on *what* we're talking about, we'll talk past each other, and that isn't dialogue.
- Dickman reads "subject" as **the subject of a sentence**:

> **A fusion of horizons is realized when we find shared predicates that fit the subject.**

- A genuine question is **not a judgment**, and **not the negation** of one. **Gadamer:** it "stands closer to a statement than any of the other linguistic phenomena, and yet it allows no logic in the sense of a logic of [assertions]."
- It's **surplus-driven**: "more in awe at the possibilities before any specific possibility is tried out."
- **With a shared question, I can try out predicative possibilities for a subject. When one works, it expands my horizon and enriches my world.**
- Genuine questions are **articulate listening signals**. They tell others we hear and understand them and want to keep talking. Questioning together **sets the horizon within which meaning can be transferred**.
- Hearing the other, making room for them in discursive space, and **holding off on resolving the matter** all come **before** the "striving to reach a resolution" that typical interrogatives have.

### Horizons and language

- **Horizons** are the fields of intelligibility that shape our forms of life, articulated in language games. **We come to share a world because we come to share a language.**
- "**Being that can be understood is language.**"
- **Gadamer:** "It is not that understanding is subsequently put into words; rather… [understanding] is **the coming-into-language of the thing itself**."
- **Gail Weiss's caution:** "sedimentation of everyday experience into recognizable patterns can serve to **codify oppression** as readily as it can promote… existential stability… one must be attentive to… those aspects of the world that **cannot be rendered intelligible within established horizons**, and that therefore demand new ways of thinking, feeling, and being."
- Dialogue **resists stagnation**. New predicates for shared subjects open **ever-broadening horizons**.
- **Why breadth matters:** horizons put what's right in front of us **in proportion**. *Intense anger* at an experience can look different later, from a broader view. **Questioning's indeterminacy can loosen even "righteously justified" anger.**

> **"We can share a determination through having first shared an indeterminacy."**

### Tradition as an event

- Languages have histories, prejudices are effects of history, and horizons are shaped by tradition. (People raised Buddhist tend to become Buddhist. People raised Muslim tend to become Muslim.)
- **Tradition is a dynamic, dialectical process of sedimentation *and* innovation** (Gadamer; Ricoeur; Bell). The word usually suggests only the static side:
  - "**traditional marriage**" invoked against marriage equality
  - **Confucius** labeled a "traditional thinker" with outdated values
  - philosophers arguing for "**the traditional god**" (Dickman 2018c)
- Traditions are **historically effected practices** structured by symbolic and mythic conventions, responding to contingencies, and contingencies are often what spark innovation.
  - **Musical performance:** each performance changes the history of a piece (acoustics, instruments, skill). Sometimes everything comes together and a performance **sets a new standard**.
  - **Qur'anic recitation** traditions show the same thing (Sells 2007).
- **Reaching understanding is less an act of a subjective ego than an *event of tradition*** (Gadamer). This links back to Ch. 1, where Aristotle and Spinoza described the thinker as reflecting a god's thinking.
- **Improvisation example:** music students research improvisation to get better at it, without noticing that **their question about improvisation was itself improvised**. That shows how subtle the interplay of sediment and innovation is.

### Situatedness vs. "objectivity"

- **What seems worth asking about is already an effect of history** (Gadamer).
- Politicians, scientists, and professors appeal to "facts that speak for themselves," as if we could stand outside history. **Propositionalism leads us to treat mere expositions as valid reasoning.** We hide from ourselves that our views are "situated in the web of historical effects." **Our views depend on "the legitimacy of the questions asked."**
- **Gadamer:** "To acquire a horizon means that one learns to look beyond what is close at hand—**not in order to look away from it but to see it better**, within a larger whole and in truer proportion."

**Reflective arguments (formal refutations) lack historical proportion:**

| Target | Formal refutation | Why it misses the point |
|---|---|---|
| **Skepticism** ("There is no truth") | Self-referentially incoherent, since the claim presents itself as true | **Formally correct, but shows no superior insight.** It tells us nothing (Gadamer) |
| **Relativism** ("What's true for me is true for me, and what's true for you is true for you") | Self-referentially incoherent, since it assumes it's universally true | Both views **keep returning in new forms** (Levinas). They point to a truth the refutation misses: **there are multiple fields of intelligibility** |

- These arguments "have the feeling of someone **trying to bowl us over**," and that show of strength actually signals weakness. They also suggest that reaching a conclusion is merely a subjective act, when **understanding is an effect and event of traditions**.

### There are no closed horizons

- "Fusion" might suggest **two separate horizons**, mine and yours, merging. That would imply horizons can be **closed**.
- **A closed horizon contradicts the situated nature of horizons** (Warnke 2016). **Horizons move as we move.**
- Reaching understanding isn't empathically entering another's horizon. **We understand what they say in response to living questions raised by historical situations.**
- **"In this way, we are all part of one great horizon."**
- Fusing horizons, we "**rise to a higher universality**" (Gadamer). **Hermeneutic universality** isn't eternal metaphysical truth. It's **complete thoughts and their questionings that ever larger swaths of humanity can share**.

### Transformation and risk

- **How can we tell whether we're listening well?** When **the other recognizes their own view in our questions.** Can we ask without slanting? Can we restate their answers without subtly undercutting them?
- **Gadamer:** one must "keep something at a distance—namely everything that suggests itself, on the basis of one's own prejudices, as the meaning expected."
- **Ricoeur's "productive distantiation":** all understanding involves alienation. Even if all understanding is self-understanding, **we always understand differently, if we understand at all.**
- **"Responsible understanding involves coming to speak otherwise than I have ever spoken before."** If speaking differently reflects *being* differently, how far might one become otherwise? **Completely**, if loyal to responsible understanding.
- That leaves **a hard choice: loyalty to understanding, or commitment to my current identity?**
- **We're transformed into a communion in which we're no longer the same person** (Gadamer). Treating our perspective as revisable means treating **our selves as revisable**.
  - **Beatty:** listening with openness requires "not merely the willingness to rework and rethink experience… but **the willingness to rework character**."
- **Every time we listen, our selfhood is at stake.**

### "You" and "I" become "us": dialogue as play

- **Gadamer:** "**All playing is a being played.**" Games draw us in with a spirit, like **team spirit**, that **surpasses individual intentionalities** and makes us parts of a greater whole.
- **Gadamer:** "A [dialogue] does not simply carry one person's opinion through against another's… Genuine [dialogue] **transforms the viewpoint of both**… one **cannot fall back into the disagreement that touched it off**… the shared interpretation of the world which makes **moral and social solidarity** possible."
- **Seesaw:** a "good match" is the **uninhibited to-and-fro**. Nothing is more excessive than play.
- **Absorption** is "an ecstatic self-forgetting that is experienced not as a loss of self-possession, but as **the free buoyancy of an elevation above oneself**."
- **Recreation:** we literally **re-create ourselves** in games. The task constrains the field of action but **feels liberating**, because **constraints are the condition for playful variety**.
- The goal is met **not by winning or solving** but by **exhibiting the dynamic form of the game**. That's unlike debate's winners and losers. Players "**spend**" themselves on the task, and **the game takes a stand in and through them**.

> **"Dialogue presents dialogue."**

**The seesaw model of dialogue:**

```
                 questions & responses (to-and-fro)
         ◀───────────────────────────────────────────▶
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  partner A                                partner B  │   ← plank
   └──────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────┘
                              ▲
                       SUBJECT MATTER   ← fulcrum: regulates and guides
```

- **The subject matter is the fulcrum.** Questions and responses are the shifts of body weight that produce the fluid back-and-forth.
- **Like good actors disappearing into their roles, dialogue partners disappear and the subject matter "speaks volumes through them."** We want to find out what ***it*** has to say to and through us.

### Shared intentionality (Searle)

- **Pushing a car together:** it isn't "I" pushing and "you" pushing. It's **our** pushing. If I find out you were faking, **I was wrong about what *I* was doing too.**
- **Searle:** "In collective behaviors, such as [dialogues], individual intentionality is **derived from** the collective intentionality."
- Individual **I-intentions** join into a common **we-intention**.
- **Contrast:** solitary hikers sheltering from a storm in the same bunker share *the same* intentions, but that's **a sum of individual actions**, not collective intentionality.
- **The understanding reached in dialogue is more than the sum of its parts. We accomplish understanding and dialogue *with* others, not by ourselves.** Coming together this way is **the aim of dialogue as a discursive practice**.

## Chapter summary in one paragraph

Questioning turns **conversation perceived** into **dialogue understood**, and it does so *with others*. **Debate** (a "battle to the bitter end") is adversarial. It polices intelligibility, defends fixed positions, and stops at conclusions. **Dialogue** has an "inner infinity" and only ever pauses. It doesn't mean being absorbed by the other either, since "we bring our own shoes." The worst conversation stoppers are **bias**, **bad faith**, and above all **propositionalist ideology**, which can't be argued down and can only be exposed through **genuine questioning**, the opening for tradition's innovation. **Genuine questioning is a mode of listening**: receptive, active, spoken (response tokens, "Is this what you mean?"), hospitable to the answerer, and aimed at **what others say, not at the others themselves**. "We listen with our mouths," and receiving such a question means "hearing ourselves being heard." Because understanding a question means asking it, **another's question becomes *our* question**. That difference-preserving relationship **transfers meaning**, respects others as co-explorers, keeps us focused on the **subject matter**, and requires **translation** across language games. Reaching an understanding is a **fusion of horizons**: finding **shared predicates fitting a shared subject**. It is an **event of tradition** (sediment and innovation), not an ego's choice. It loosens prejudice and anger by **sharing an indeterminacy first**. It rises to a **hermeneutic universality**, puts our very **selves at risk** of transformation, and works like a **game of seesaw** in which the subject matter is the fulcrum, "you" and "I" become "us," and **dialogue presents dialogue**.

## Instructor's suggestions (Appendix, pp. 218–219)

Alongside the ["three things"](../00-Word-Phrase-Sentence-Strategy/README.md) protocol:

1. **Think, pair, share:** *Why do good conversations come to an end?*
2. **Telephone transcript:** give students a transcript of a phone call and have them **identify every way the speakers show they're listening**, through **listening tokens** ("mmhm…") or **questions**.
3. **Distinguish empathy (or sympathy) from understanding**, in the book's technical sense:
   - **Empathy** = being able to feel like another person. It **may not require understanding**.
   - **Understanding** = grasping the complete thought in what another person says. It **doesn't require empathizing**.
   - *Think, pair, share* on the case: "**As a woman, men will never understand my experience, what it is like to be me.**"
4. **Think, pair, share:** *What happens to another person's question when we ask it too?*

## Discussion questions

- Has your school or workplace ever held a "dialogue" competition? What would one look like, and how would you judge it?
- Find three response tokens you used in your last conversation. What was each one doing?
- Pick a text you struggled with. What was the author's *question*? Is it also yours?
- When did a conversation change you so much that you couldn't "fall back into the disagreement that touched it off"?
