## What Bloom's Taxonomy is

Benjamin Bloom built a taxonomy of **measurable verbs** to describe and classify observable knowledge, skills, attitudes, behaviors, and abilities. The idea: certain **observable actions** show that cognitive activity is happening. If a learning objective uses a measurable verb, it says exactly what a student has to *do* to show they've learned.

The handout uses Bloom's **original six levels**, from lowest to highest:

```
                    ▲  EVALUATION      make and defend judgments
                   ▲▲  SYNTHESIS       build a new whole
                  ▲▲▲  ANALYSIS        break it into parts
                 ▲▲▲▲  APPLICATION     use it in a real situation
                ▲▲▲▲▲  COMPREHENSION   understand the facts
               ▲▲▲▲▲▲  KNOWLEDGE       remember it
```

The handout calls the upper levels "verbs that demonstrate critical thinking."

> **Not in the handout:** a 2001 revision by Anderson and Krathwohl renamed the levels as verbs (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, **Evaluate, Create**). It put creating at the top, above evaluating. You'll often see that version too.

## The six levels

| Level | Definition (p. 2) | Typical verbs | Sample question stems (pp. 5–6) | What students produce (p. 7) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge** | Remember previously learned information | define, list, name, recall, state, identify | "What is…?" · "Who was it that…?" · "Which is true or false…?" | Recall of events, people, text readings, films |
| **Comprehension** | Demonstrate an understanding of the facts | explain, paraphrase, summarize, restate, distinguish, give examples | "Can you write in your own words…?" · "Can you provide an example of what you mean…?" · "What differences exist between…?" | Summary, outline, own statement, analogy, causal relationships |
| **Application** | Apply knowledge to actual situations | apply, use, solve, demonstrate, illustrate, construct | "Do you know another instance where…?" · "Can you apply the method used to some experience of your own…?" · "What questions would you ask of…?" | Diagram, map, illustration, forecast, project |
| **Analysis** | Break down objects or ideas into simpler parts and find evidence to support generalizations | analyze, compare, contrast, differentiate, categorize, **question** | "What was the underlying problem with…?" · "Why did … changes occur?" · "What do you see as other possible outcomes?" | **Argument**, **syllogism broken down**, conclusion checked, parts of propaganda, questionnaire |
| **Synthesis** | Compile component ideas into a new whole or propose alternative solutions | create, design, compose, formulate, devise, propose | "What would happen if…?" · "Can you see a possible solution to…?" · "Can you develop a proposal which would…?" | **Question**, hypothesis, invention, set of rules, alternative course of action |
| **Evaluation** | Make and defend judgments based on internal evidence or external criteria | judge, justify, critique, argue, assess, defend | "Can you defend your position about…?" · "Is there a better solution to…?" · "Do you think … is a good or a bad thing?" | **Group discussion**, court trial, self-evaluation, recommendation, valuing |

### Words to avoid in objectives (p. 8)

The handout says these are too vague to measure, so they shouldn't appear in a learning objective:

- **Words:** believe, capacity, comprehend, conceptualize, experience, feel, hear, intelligence, know, listen, memorize, perceive, realize, recognize, see, self-actualize, **think**, **understand**
- **Phrases:** "appreciation for," "awareness of," "familiar with," "interest in," "knowledge of," "understanding of," and similar

So instead of "Students will *understand* the argument," write "Students will *paraphrase* the argument" or "*identify* its premises."

### Two quirks worth noticing

1. **The same verb shows up at several levels.** *Compare* appears under Comprehension, Analysis, Synthesis, and Evaluation. *Question* appears under Analysis on pp. 1–2 and as a Synthesis product on p. 7. A verb alone doesn't fix the level. What the student actually does with it does.
2. **The handout contradicts itself in a few places.** *Recognize* and *memorize* are on the "avoid" list (p. 8) but are listed as Knowledge verbs on pp. 2 and 7. *Comprehend* is on the avoid list but is a level heading on p. 3.

## Bloom's and *Using Questions to Think*

### Dickman names Bloom directly (Ch. 2, p. 30)

In Chapter 2 Dickman describes exactly the advice this handout gives. Teachers are told to ask "higher-order" questions rather than "lower-order" ones, and to "employ Benjamin Bloom's taxonomy of cognitive learning objectives (or some version of it) and to try to correlate kinds of questions with kinds of learning outcomes." He sums it up: "It is as if a student needs to be prompted to evaluate or synthesize information by evaluative or synthesis questions."

He pushes back in three ways:

| Bloom's handout assumes… | Dickman's response |
|---|---|
| Higher-level question stems push thinking to higher levels ("ensure your students' thinking progresses to higher levels," p. 3) | Many people equate open questions with higher-order thinking. On the level of grammar, that's "mere myth." Ask "How was your day at school today?" and you still get one word (pp. 30–32) |
| The *kind* of question (by level) is what matters | What matters more is whether a question is **neutral or loaded**. A higher-level stem can still be loaded: "Do you think … is a good or a bad thing?" offers just two answers, and "Can you defend your position about…?" assumes you have one |
| Questions exist to get students to *show* something | That's the **epistemic imperative** in exam form: "Make me know that you know X" (p. 34). These are **sincere** questions, and they're fine. But **genuine** questions, asked to wonder together, don't belong to any level |

### "Think," "understand," and "listen" are on the avoid list

The words the handout says to avoid are the words Dickman's book is about: **think**, **understand**, **listen**, **know**, **perceive**, **comprehend**. That doesn't make the handout wrong. The two are doing different jobs:

- **Bloom's** asks: *how can a teacher see that learning happened?* So it uses verbs for things you can observe.
- **Dickman** asks: *what is it to think, reason, and understand at all?* His answer is that understanding a sentence means grasping it as an answer to a question, which nobody can watch from outside.

Dickman's own classroom method meets Bloom's halfway. His **word-phrase-sentence** strategy ([folder 00](../00-Word-Phrase-Sentence-Strategy/README.md)) has students *select*, *cite*, and *explain* why something stood out, which are all measurable verbs. But he uses it to start a dialogue, not to grade one.

### Questioning comes before every level

Bloom puts *question* inside one level, as a skill among others. For Dickman, questioning has **hermeneutic priority**. It's the condition for understanding anything at all (Ch. 3). Even recalling a fact at the Knowledge level means grasping that fact as an answer to some question.

### Where the two line up

The overlaps are real, and Bloom's can be a useful checklist for the book's skills:

| Bloom's level | Where it shows up in the book |
|---|---|
| **Comprehension** | Turning a perceived **sentence** into an understood **complete thought** (Ch. 1–2) |
| **Analysis** | Breaking a complete thought into **subject, copula, predicate** (Ch. 1). Breaking an **exposition** into **premises, inference, conclusion** (Ch. 4). The handout's "syllogism broken down" and "argument" are Part II's material |
| **Synthesis** | **Synthesis** of thesis and antithesis in dialogue (Ch. 7). Building **worldviews** from symbols and metaphors (Ch. 6) |
| **Evaluation** | **Rational critique** (Ch. 6). Validity, soundness, strength, cogency (Ch. 5) |

One difference stands out. The handout lists **group discussion** and **court trial** side by side as Evaluation products. For Dickman they're opposites: a trial is **debate**, adversarial and aimed at a verdict, while a discussion can be **dialogue**, which never really concludes (Ch. 8).

## Bloom's-style study questions for this book

These questions are ours. They use the handout's stems and verbs and apply them to the book.

| Level | Study questions |
|---|---|
| **Knowledge** | What is a complete thought? · Name the three domains of the book's scaffold. · What does "copula" mean? |
| **Comprehension** | In your own words, what is the difference between a sincere and a genuine question? · Can you give an example of a loaded question and rewrite it to be neutral? · Explain why Dickman says you can't "win" an argument. |
| **Application** | Take a question you asked today and run it through the rewrite test. Was it sincere or genuine? · Find a sentence in a news article and state the question it answers. · Use the four reasoning orientations ("And?", "But why?", …) on an argument from class. |
| **Analysis** | Break a paragraph from an op-ed into premises and conclusion. Is it an argument or just an exposition? · Compare debate and dialogue: what is each one aiming at? · What does "Has the bus left already?" presuppose that "Has the bus left yet?" doesn't? |
| **Synthesis** | Write three genuine questions about a topic you care about. · Propose a classroom discussion format that would encourage shared asking. · Two classmates disagree. Write a question that could lead toward a fusion of horizons. |
| **Evaluation** | Is Dickman right that "higher-order" questions are a myth? Defend your position. · Using rational critique, assess one argument from Chapter 5. · Should a teacher ever ask a question they already know the answer to? Justify your answer. |

## Sample learning objectives (with measurable verbs)

Also ours, written the way the handout recommends:

1. **Identify** the subject, copula, and predicate of a given sentence. *(Knowledge)*
2. **Paraphrase** the fundamental axiom of Chapter 2 in one sentence. *(Comprehension)*
3. **Classify** five questions as sincere or genuine using the rewrite test. *(Application)*
4. **Differentiate** an argument from an exposition in a short passage. *(Analysis)*
5. **Formulate** a thesis-centered, an antithesis-centered, and a synthesis-centered question for a class dispute. *(Synthesis)*
6. **Critique** an argument using at least two of the six ways of rational critique. *(Evaluation)*
