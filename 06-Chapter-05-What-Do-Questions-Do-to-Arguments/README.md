## The big idea

Two guiding questions: **What is the structure of reasoning?** And **how does questioning relate to rationally ordered arguments?**

- Standard logic courses teach us to assess sentence relations **independently of content or meaning**, that is, apart from the questions the sentences answer. **Validity is formal**, and you don't need to understand the sentences to check it. On that view, validity has nothing to do with questioning.
- Dickman's position: **rationality** is what makes the difference between a list of sentences (an exposition) and an **argument**.
- Rationality is structured by **laws or rules**, and those rules are what make *free* thinking possible.
- **Reasoning runs in two directions.** It moves forward toward **conclusions** and backward toward **fundamental premises or grounds**. Every answer raises another "**But why?**", and the chain ends at Kant's **unconditioned**.

> Just as **thought aims at unity** (subject + predicate through the copula), **reason aims at unity** (premises + conclusions). The pursuit of unity in difference comes **before** the distinction between discovery and invention (Ricoeur).

Deep questions of this kind: *Is the soul immortal? Is there room for free will? Is there a divine being?*

## 1. Rational inferences are valid or strong (pp. 90–100)

### Rationality and inference

- **Rationality** = a theory of what counts as **good reasoning** in general, and of **following inferences** in particular.
- **Inference** = a further complete thought drawn from, or developed on the basis of, another one.
  - A conclusion **"follows from"** a premise, and a premise **"leads to"** a conclusion.
  - The support is **inferential support**. The derivation is **inferential derivation**.
- **Etymology:** *in-* (bringing into awareness) + *-ference* (as in trans*fer*ence, *fer*ries). To infer is to **carry a new complete thought into consciousness on the basis of another**, carrying the premise's **truth** across with it.
- **Not causal.** In some arguments the conclusion is **already there** in the premise, the way the mind fills in a **dotted-line triangle**. **Reasoning uncovers the conclusion from the ground of the premise.**

### Immediate inferences (no mediating premise)

| Name | Meaning | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conversion** | "to turn around" | No X are Y ⟹ No Y are X | "No people are mere things" ⟹ "No mere things are people"; "No cats are dogs" ⟹ "No dogs are cats" |
| **Obversion** | "to reveal a different perspective"; change quality | No X are non-Y ⟹ All X are Y | "No people are non-humans" ⟹ "All people are humans" |

If the premise is true, there is sufficient reason to believe the conclusion. These inferences **preserve truth**.

### Formal vs. informal logic; argument as a genre

- **Formal logic** studies only the abstract order of inferences.
- **Informal logic** studies reasoning in ordinary language: equivocations, definitions, warrants for assumptions, and fallacies in practice.
- **Arguments compile complete thoughts, together with the questions they answer, into coherent wholes.**
- **Argument is a *genre* of discourse**, like iambic poetry or autobiography. **Logic is the set of principles governing this genre.**
  - Other genres have structure too. Fairytales have a beginning, middle, and end, plus **magic or enchantment**.
  - **Inference defines argument the way enchantment defines the fairytale.** Remove inference and the exposition no longer fits the genre.

### One rationality or many?

| Motive for universality | Motive for particularity |
|---|---|
| We want to **reason with people across cultures** | **Western models of rationality** have been complicit with **colonialism, oppression, sexism, racism** (Herbjørnsrud 2017) |

- **A model of rationality isn't rationality itself**, just as a model airplane isn't an airplane. Rejecting a colonial *model* doesn't make rationality *as such* colonial.
- **Hold the tension:** keep open the possibility of a universal structure, and temper it with **tentative, provisional, self-correcting humility**. The structure of reasoning is itself something we can reason about together.
- Dickman is drawn to **Pyrrhonian skepticism**, which suspends loyalty to either side, but notes that this skepticism comes out of Plato and Aristotle. It may be "the most Western form of 'rationality.'"
- Critical race, feminist, queer, and **crip theorists** have shown that "rational" has partly been a **social construct complicit with white-supremacist ableism and Eurocentric patriarchy** (McFague 1982; Kafer 2013).
- **The choice:** a **radical revolution** that rejects inherited rationality as beyond saving, or a **radical reformation** that revises it to be inclusive and accessible. **Dickman bets on reformation**, and allows that experience may push him toward revolution.

### Three kinds of reasoning

```
                     ┌── valid / invalid ─── + true premises ──▶ SOUND     (certainty)
   DEDUCTION ────────┤
                     └── from axioms, via laws of logic

   INDUCTION ──────── strong / weak ────── + true premises ──▶ COGENT    (probability)

   ABDUCTION ──────── inference to the best explanation                   (plausibility)
```

#### Deduction

- The **strictest** kind: it gives **certainty**. Deduction argues for true conclusions **from axioms** (statements assumed true from the start), and preserves truth **through validity**.
- **Laws it must respect:**
  - **Law of Excluded Middle:** for any complete thought, either it or its opposite is true.
  - **Law of Non-Contradiction:** two contradictory complete thoughts joined by "both/and" can't be true *in the same sense at the same time*.
- **Two levels of evaluation:**
  1. **Form:** if the premises were true, could the conclusion be false? If not, the argument is **formally valid**. If it could, it's **invalid**.
  2. **Content:** are the premises actually true?

**Categorical syllogism** (exactly two premises; statements of the forms *All X are Y / No X are Y / Some X are Y / Some X are not Y*):

```
Premise 1:  All philosophers are wise.        All P are W.
Premise 2:  Socrates is a philosopher.        All S are P.
Conclusion: Therefore, Socrates is wise.      Therefore, all S are W.
```

- Using **substitution instances** (letters) lets us learn the **abstract pattern** and **transfer** it to new arguments.
- **Validity concerns only the pattern, not the content.** An argument can be valid even if *every* premise and the conclusion are false.
- An **invalid** attempt **breaks the rules of deductive rationality**. It's a **failed inference**. *"To use an invalid argument is, essentially, not to reason. It is to do something irrational."*
- **Sound** = valid form **+** true premises. This is "**the highest endorsement we can give to an argument**."
- **Truth and falsity apply only to complete thoughts. Validity applies only to arguments.** (Not "that's a valid point" or "your feelings are valid.")

**True premises + true conclusion can still be invalid**, because the premises are irrelevant:

```
Premise 1:  Bachelors are unmarried men.
Premise 2:  The horizon is where the sky and ground meet in the distance.
Conclusion: Therefore, competitive games undermine cooperation.
```

That's an **exposition**, a pile of sentences, not an argument.

**Vetted valid forms** to learn: *modus ponens*, **disjunctive syllogism**, **hypothetical syllogism** (Bassham et al. 2019; Tidman & Kahane 1999).

**Spotting invalidity by substitution:**

```
All A are B.            All apples are fruit.         (true)
All C are B.            All tomatoes are fruit.       (true)
∴ All A are C.          ∴ All apples are tomatoes.    (false!)
```

**Consistency:**

- Thoughts are **consistent** when they are neither **contrary** (both can't be true, though both can be false) nor **contradictory** (exactly one is true).
- Consistency is **necessary but not sufficient** for validity.
- Inconsistency is **not necessary** for invalidity. A consistent pattern can still be invalid.

**Surface inconsistency can hide a deeper consistency:**

- **Hypocrisy** = a *practical* inconsistency between actions and beliefs.
- Yet **poetry, religious paradox, and Socratic irony** can point to a deeper unity:
  - Jesus: don't let your **left hand know what your right hand is doing**, which suggests that with subjectivity comes a right to deceive (Kierkegaard).
  - **Socrates:** all I know is that I know nothing, and this is what makes him the wisest.
- To bring out the underlying rationality, attend to **ambiguities and equivocations**.

#### Induction

- **All inductive arguments are technically deductively invalid.** Induction concerns **probability**, not certainty. The conclusion *probably* follows and *probably* preserves the premises' truth.

```
Premise:    All animals with sharp teeth that I have seen so far eat meat.
Conclusion: Thus, the sharp-toothed beast before me probably eats meat as well.

Premise:    The sun has risen every morning so far.
Conclusion: Thus, it will probably rise tomorrow morning.
```

- **Strong or weak**, not valid or invalid. Strength depends on the **weight of evidence**, **sample quality and size**, the **plausibility of the generalization**, and how coherently the elements combine into a complete picture (like a **trial lawyer**'s case).
- Not only particular → general. Induction can also go **past → future**, **general → specific**, or **general → general**. It is fundamental to the natural sciences.
- **The problem of induction:** our confidence that the past guides the future **rests on past observation**, so it's **circular**. But that judges induction by **deductive standards external to it**.
- **All reasoning is fallible, in different ways:**
  - **Deduction** can go wrong **only through content** (a false premise).
  - **Induction** can go wrong that way too, and also: **even with true premises and a strong relation, the conclusion is never guaranteed.**
- **Cogent** = strong **+** true premises. This is the highest praise for induction. It is never a "proof."

**Two kinds of probability:**

| Objective probability | Subjective probability |
|---|---|
| A **real indeterminacy in the world**. The pattern is likely, but undetermined until it happens | A **judgment of likelihood** given one's epistemic position: **personal confidence** |
| | e.g., a couple commits long-term with **subjective confidence** despite **objective uncertainty** (one may leave or die) |

When information is incomplete we still have to decide. Example: **college administrators in spring 2020 facing COVID-19**: "What happens if we do nothing? If we suspend classes for two weeks?"

**Three inductive patterns:**

| Pattern | What it does | Key tests / cautions |
|---|---|---|
| **Causal analysis** | Explains how or why something happened, assigns responsibility, predicts or controls. A **cause** = an initial condition, or one of many sufficient conditions (Conway & Munson 2000) | **Chilly house:** the house itself is a **constant background condition**. Look for **unusual, varying** factors: the proximate or triggering cause (furnace broken? turned off?). Compare with past cases and run tests |
| **Analogy** | Two things alike in many ways are probably alike in one more (Weston 2009) | Are they **relevantly** similar? (If identical, it's identity, not analogy.) **Apples and oranges** *are* similar if the point is "non-poisonous, roundish fruit" |
| **Generalization** | From specific examples to a more universal conclusion | Use **more than one** example (one only *illustrates*). Use a **representative sample**. Watch statistics that are **suspiciously precise**. **Counterexamples refine:** "All fast food is unhealthy" → Subway → "**Most** fast food is unhealthy" |

#### Abduction

- **Inference to the best explanation** for our purposes, given what we know so far.
- **Sherlock Holmes** says "Deduction, my dear Watson!" but actually uses **abduction**.
- **Criteria** (Baggini & Fosl 2010): **simplicity**, **coherence** with what we already know, **predictive power**, **comprehensiveness**.
- Dickman notes that Part II is itself an abductive attempt at a **better explanation** of how questioning relates to reasoning.

> These patterns, from validity to generalization to abduction, give us **a vocabulary for questioning arguments** and the **structures within which reasoning can happen**.

## 2. Rational laws set thinking free (pp. 100–107)

### The paradox

- "Free thinking" has been taken over by groups like the **Freethinkers Society**, usually set against religion or "indoctrination." That isn't the sense meant here. **Here, freethinking concerns only how questioning relates to complete thoughts and to reasoning.**
- If thinking is governed by laws (subject + predicate, validity, abduction), how can it be free? What happens when we "break" logical laws? What does "**think for yourself!**" even mean?

> **Laws set us free.** (Dickman notes this runs against John 8:32, or at least Paul in Romans 7:6.)

- Remove the **rules of the road** and you can't get anywhere.
- Remove the **rules of basketball** and you can't play.
- Remove ***mitzvot*** or ***Shariah*** and people can't be free to *be* Jewish or Muslim.
- Someone roaming aimlessly might find divine instructions liberating: "Thank God I know what to do now!"
- Not every law is fundamental. Some should be **dismantled**, as US **Civil Rights** activists showed with laws that perpetuate white supremacy.

### Constitutive rules vs. regulative ideals

| | **Constitutive rules** | **Regulative ideals** |
|---|---|---|
| Function | The **basic framework** within which an activity is *possible at all* | Norms that **adapt** to changing circumstances or values |
| Examples | Rules of driving, *Shariah* for being Muslim, the **US Constitution** for being a US citizen, **logical laws** (validity, non-contradiction) | Developed later in this chapter and in Ch. 6 |
| Remove them and… | **the possibility of that way of being disappears** | — |

**Logical laws are constitutive rules for the freedom to think and reason at all.** Without them, you aren't really thinking.

### Contradiction, tautology, and self-referential incoherence

- **Law of non-contradiction** (Aristotle, *Metaphysics* 1005b19–30): a complete thought and its negation can't both be true in the same sense at the same time. It's often called **the foundation of all logic**.
  - A contradiction isn't just *hard* to think. **You aren't thinking yet.** Contradictions are **aborted attempts at reasoning**, impossible for two reasons:
    1. **It takes time** to think a complete thought, to predicate of a subject.
    2. The supposedly **simultaneous** opposed thoughts **cancel each other out**.
- **Tautology** is the opposite bookend: necessarily true in every possible world. "**Either it is snowing, or it is not snowing.**"
  - **Contradiction** = opposing claims **conjoined** ("and"). **Tautology** = opposing claims **disjoined** ("or"). Swapping the coordinating conjunction turns one into the other.
- **Self-referential incoherence** (Dickman's favorite boundary): "**No perspective is correct.**"
  - The sentence is itself a perspective, so it undermines itself.
  - It *looks* like an answer to a question, so it seems like a complete thought. But because we can't understand contradictions, **it's an aborted answer to a question no one can ever authentically ask.**
- Does that mean we're "not free" to think it? **No. We've reached the boundary of thinking.** In contradicting ourselves we're not thinking freely, because we're **not thinking at all**.
- Demanding the "freedom" to think contradictions **confuses crossing constitutive boundaries with spontaneity**. **Freethinking is patterned, not random.**
- **Free-verse poetry** needn't follow the constitutive rules of thinking. **Reasoning is a different genre.**

### Practicing the rules perfects freedom

- As with music and games, **learning the laws of logic and practicing them perfects our freedom** to think and reason. Recognizing ***modus ponens*** makes you a freer reasoner.
- **Appropriating** the rules through practice makes arguments **our own** instead of **parroting** them.

**Isaiah Berlin's two liberties:**

| Negative liberty | Positive liberty |
|---|---|
| **Freedom *from*** restraint (e.g., winning the right to vote) | **Freedom *to*** do something (e.g., with basketball's rules in place, we're free to play as we please) |

The laws of reason give **positive** liberty. **By appropriating the rules, we become *responsible* for our thoughts and arguments.** It's no longer just *an* argument, complete thought, or question. It's **my** argument, **my** complete thought, **my** question. And since arguments build **lifeworlds and worldviews**, learning argumentation helps us **make worldviews our own**.

### Fallacies: "outlaw" attempts at thought

Breaking the laws of reason means **not even thinking**. These "outlaw" attempts are **fallacies**.

#### Formal fallacies (errors of form)

```
VALID: modus ponens                      INVALID: affirming the consequent
P1: If it is raining, the road is wet.   P1: If it is raining, the road is wet.
P2: It is raining.  (affirm antecedent)  P2: The road is wet.  (affirm consequent)
∴   The road is wet.                     ∴   It is raining.    ✗ (sprinklers? spill?)
```

- With a formal fallacy **we fail to reason because of the form alone**. We may *perceive* the exposition, *intend* an argument, even *believe* we reasoned, but **we can't understand it as an argument**.
- In casual talk we call these "bad arguments." **Technically, they aren't arguments.**

#### Informal fallacies (errors of content)

Three general families:

1. **Ambiguity / the meaning of terms**
2. **Presumption / assuming too much**
3. **Relevance / changing the topic**

**Why we fall for them:** **biases** rooted in projected priorities and regulative ideals, such as **wishful thinking**, **confirmation bias**, demanding **one cause** or "**the**" answer, and **inappropriate appeals to authority** (your doctor drives a Mercedes, so we all should; **Instagram models** giving dietary advice).

**"Begging the question" (correctly understood):**

- Common misuse: "that raises a new question."
- Technically: supporting a claim with a **controversial assumption** instead of a fitting premise, where **the premise and conclusion say basically the same thing**.
- Example: "**Ambidextrous hitters are the best [conclusion] because solely left- or right-handed hitters do not hit as well [premise].**" It's formally valid, but the content gives no relevant support.

**Ad hominem** ("to the person"): changing the topic from the argument to the person, usually to degrade them. "*Of course you'd say that, you're just trying to win.*" Wanting to win doesn't make someone's reasoning wrong. **The person making the ad hominem has traded reasoning for fighting.**

> In all these ways we break the laws of reasoning **and so lose our freedom to think**.

### The unfreedom of "freethinking"

- **"Think for yourself!"** has a **fetish character** in modern Western societies. If you start thinking for yourself *because you were told to*, are you thinking for yourself or obeying? **Adorno:** freethinking is a **parody** of thinking.
- **Militant atheists** set "free thought" rigidly against "brainwashing," as if there were a space of reasoning untouched by any formation. That's **"freethinking ideology."**
- **The rote objection:** "Subjects and predicates are an *obligation* on my thinking. I can think in **pictures**!"
  - **Imagining ≠ thinking.** Imagining happens in pictures, thinking in concepts (Descartes). You can picture a triangle and count its angles. You **can't picture a thousand-sided figure (chiliagon)**, yet you can still work out its angles.
  - The objection's real problem is its **attitude**: it treats laws as **obligations to escape**. That parallels how many students experience college, as something they **have to** do rather than **get to** do. This is the US attitude behind the **social degradation of the liberal arts**.

**The worst form of freethinking ideology is propositionalism** (Meyer):

- Reasoning and thinking **cut off from questioning entirely**. Logical relations treated as concerning only sentences, unrelated to questions we're asking. Claims treated as true or false **regardless of the questions they address**.
- It creates a space where we're **subjectively convinced** we reason freely (Adorno), and it's exactly there, **succumbing to an ideology**, that our "thinking" is held back by the forces we wanted to escape.

**Pseudo-thinking in culture:**

| Phenomenon | Dickman's critique |
|---|---|
| **Twitter** | Constant demands to share what you "think," to make a **judgment** |
| **Collective shaming** | Thoughts silenced by collective disapproval instead of engagement. (Some things *are* shameful, like white supremacy or naïveté about white privilege.) Example: **2016**, Sanders and Stein supporters shamed for "betrayal." **Agreeing with the shamers isn't thinking freely** |
| **Reviews culture** | The demand to have a take on every movie or song |
| **Commodified reasoning** | Market competition among basic-reasoning textbooks: Dicarlo's *How to Become a Really Good Pain in the Ass*, Frankfurt's *On Bullshit* (Hamby 2013). The industry **portrays reasoning as "being a pain in the ass"** |
| **Crash-course expectations** | Miracles expected from reasoning courses (law-school prep, the meaning of life) |
| **Perennial laments** | "Kids these days can't think." Employers want "critical thinking" but **can't define it** (Korn 2014) |
| **Anti-intellectual rhetoric** | Constant cries of "**hypocrite!**" (logical and practical contradiction), which fill the media with **pseudo-reasoning, a shadow of reasoning** |

## 3. A phenomenology of incessant questions (pp. 107–115)

### "But, why?"

- **Reasoning is like a child incessantly asking "But, why?"**
- **Freire:** "There are insistent questions that we all have to ask and that make it clear to us that it is not possible to study simply for the sake of studying."
- Those questions open avenues of inference **toward conclusions or toward premises and grounds**.
- **Socrates** was the annoying **gadfly** whose stinging questions caused dizziness and uncertainty.
- "Why?" here seeks **reasons**, not mere **causal explanations**. Ultimately it seeks **fundamental reasons**, secure foundations: **the unconditioned**.

### Appeals to higher authority

- At work: managers, or organizing coworkers to change policy. Voters: single-issue Supreme Court voters. Advice from more experienced people. **Prayer to supreme beings.**
- **Reason likewise seeks ultimate authorization.** It runs **both ways**: premises → conclusions, *and* premises and conclusions → **grounds**. It reaches for a **first principle**, the condition of all conditions: **the unconditioned** (Kant).

### Three domains of the unconditioned (mapped onto the complete thought)

Not "objective" or "subjective" in the naïve sense. These are reason's searches for first principles of **the whole of nature**, **the whole of the mind**, and **the whole of both in relation** (what some call "God").

| Domain | Discipline | Part of the complete thought | Ultimate question |
|---|---|---|---|
| **World** (objective) | **Philosophical cosmology** | **Predicate** | The ultimate principle of cosmic being as a whole |
| **Self** (subjective) | **Philosophical psychology / anthropology** | **Subject** | The ultimate principle of conscious being as a whole |
| **Totality** (self + world) | **Philosophical theology / fundamental ontology** | **Copula** (being) | The ultimate unifying principle of Being itself (gods, the Dao) |

> At all three, reason reaches **impasses, not answers**, so we turn to **metaphors and symbols instead of concepts** (Ch. 6). Our questioning is **driven, or called**, by these symbolic, radiating aspects of the unconditioned.

#### (a) The world: reason "spins its wheels"

- Dickman emphasizes **relations over relata** and **predicaments over things**. Reason demands a unity of all processes. **Causality** has become the dominant way we think things are related.
- **Antinomies** (arguments that seem reasonable on both sides):

| Question | Thesis | Antithesis |
|---|---|---|
| **Beginning of the world / time** | Every effect has a cause, so there must be a **first cause** | An absolute beginning forces us to ask what came **"before" time**, which is illogical, so there's **no beginning** |
| **Free will** | There seem to be **free initiatives** (just as there seems to be a first cause) | If freedom can interrupt the causal grid at any moment, the grid is **unreliable**. It is reliable, so **no room for freedom** |

- Reason can only throw up its hands: "**Who knows?!**" **Leaps to a "first cause" are never rationally justifiable.**
- Yet the unconditioned **inspires further inquiry** into precedent conditions, parts and wholes, causality and freedom, necessity and contingency.
- **Freedom** makes sense of **actions**: we hold people responsible in ways we don't hold causes responsible. (We never ask a tree, "**Why are you choosing to lose your leaves?!**")
- At the level of the unconditioned, **we work on our fields of intelligibility, not on experience**. We don't learn more about the perceived world. We **clarify our frameworks**, and only through **analogies, symbols, metaphors, and myths** (Kant; Anderson 1998).

#### (b) The self: the elusive "I"

- Kant's **transcendental apperception** (the "I think") makes all categories and concepts possible. It's a **presupposition** of thinking, **never given as a subject matter**. We never perceive the "I."
- **The breakfast story:** tell us what you ate, and you split into **storyteller** and **the one the story is about**. Tell us the story of telling the story, and you split again. **The storyteller is like the I**: systematically elusive, never an object.
- You can't go from "I think" to "**I exist as a *thing***" (against some Cartesians). That **reifies** thinking. **Thinking is the determiner, not the determined.** The determining subject, not the determinable object.
- **Reason spins its wheels here too:**
  - The **soul as immaterial substance**: the I always seems the same, but that's a **logical feature of unified consciousness**, not the permanence of an entity (Dunne 2004). **The I thinks substances but isn't one.**
  - The **soul as an empirically identifiable person**: identifying a human as one "soul" **constructs a model**. It doesn't perceive a subjective unity.
  - The self can't be just a function of the material causal network, since that would throw us back to the unconditioned in the *world*.
- So arguments about the self can only be **a *discipline*, not a *doctrine* or dogmatism** (Kant). **The self is neither mere matter nor mere spirit.**
- **"Thinking is happening, and this is the mode of existence of the I. I do not think myself, but only subjects and predicates."** Even my **ego** isn't the "I" (Sartre).
- The laws of logic **produce concepts**, and this unfolding is **the freedom of autonomous reasoning**. Through **schemas** we generate options for predication. We can **resist** them and fail to obey rational laws, and so fail to think freely. **So reasoning is intrinsically ethical.**

#### (c) The totality: God, the Dao, the Dharmakāya

- Self and world seem radically different yet **interact**, so reason seeks their unity.
- Traditions: **Islam, Christianity, Judaism, Advaita Vedānta**; the classical Chinese **Dao**; the Buddhist ***Dharmakāya***.
- For convenience Dickman uses the Western "**perfect being**" (omniscient, omnibenevolent, omnipotent), noting that the critiques should transfer to other figures.

**Three arguments, parallel to the three parts of the complete thought:**

| Argument | Leans toward | Form | Critique |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cosmological** (Aquinas; Kant) | Cosmology | A universe of **contingent** things exists, so a **necessary** first cause exists, and that is God | A **leap from rational principle to empirical reality**. **Necessity is a logical principle of the understanding, not a feature of the world.** Causality is the mind's *a priori* synthetic structure, which we can't help extending beyond space and time. (Compare "everything" as a subject.) It ends in the **abyss of reason**: "**If a god caused everything, what caused the god?**", a natural, predictable incessancy of questioning |
| **Teleological / design** (Paley; Kant) | Psychology | Purposes and order (like a **clock**) imply a **divine clockmaker**, an ultimate Artist or Author | At most a principle generating order, **not an "intelligent designer"** creator. Another **desperate leap**. Kant: both cosmological and teleological arguments are **the ontological proof in disguise** |
| **Ontological** (**Anselm**, *Proslogion*) | Neither pole: being itself | (1) God = "**that than which nothing greater can be thought**." (2) ***Reductio ad absurdum***: deny it and you get a contradiction. (3) Existing in reality > existing only in the understanding. (4) As denying a triangle's three angles shows you don't know what you're talking about, **only a "fool" says there is no God** (Psalm 14:1) | **Existence ("being") is a function of complete thoughts, not things** (Kant). It works as a **copula**, not a **determinate predicate**: "God is is-ing." Telling actual from possible takes **experience**. Even if a triangle by definition has three angles, **no triangle need exist**. **"Necessity of thought ≠ necessity in things."** A more basic belief than "God exists" is a predication like "**My god is almighty**" |

**A reconstructive reading.** Replace "God" with "**principle**" or "**the unconditioned**." Thinking must be governed by hierarchically ordered laws, like non-contradiction. **Denying that laws apply to thinking means giving up thinking, which *is* foolish.**

**The overlooked context: a prayer.**

- Anselm speaks not *about* God but ***to*** God, in the second person: "**You** are that than which nothing greater can be conceived."
- He is in a state of **ultimate concern** (Tillich). His life's meaning and the world's are at stake. It isn't a logic puzzle done with scientific neutrality.
- Only so does the argument **unite the highest subjective and highest objective principles**. It's an **existentially involved proposition**, not a correspondence description.

### Symbols, ideals, and "god" as a predicate

- **Only symbols express ultimate concern.** Whatever you take as your ultimate concern becomes your "god" (Tillich). **Stake your life on the dollar and money is your god.** Ultimacy **transforms ordinary concepts**: a dollar is no longer just a dollar.
- Stretching "cause" from a time sequence to the **"first cause" of the universe** turns it into a **religious symbol**, not an empirical concept.
- **Religious notions of the unconditioned (gods, the Dao) are better treated as *ideals of reason* than as proper names of existing entities.**
  - **Ideals** have **practical power to guide action** without objective reality (Kant; Anderson). They're **not self-contradictory**, even though they try to gather all possible subjects and predicates.
  - Ideals are **archetypes**, **postulates of reason in practical action**. Kant calls divine ideals the "**crown of thinking**": thinking **in the service of living well**.
- **If "god" is supposed to correspond to an entity in external reality, no such being exists** (Tillich). Holding to gods as entities in space and time would mean rejecting thought itself.
- **"God" is a predicate, not a subject**, like "christ," "buddha," "prophet," "king," "president," "hero," "power forward." Jewish communities **predicate** "god" of the figure named by the **tetragrammaton**. Calling that figure "God" is like calling the current president "President."

> **Rationality has *constitutive laws* and is also structured by *regulative ideals*.** At the edge of thought and reason, concepts and categories run out, and we turn to **symbols, metaphors, and analogies** (Ch. 6).

**The point of this section:** not *why* we question at all (Ch. 3), but **what pulls us to question ever further**.

## Chapter summary in one paragraph

**Rationality** is what turns an exposition into an argument. It works through **inference**, which carries truth from premise to conclusion without causation, the conclusion being "already there" like a dotted-line triangle. Argument is a **genre** defined by inference. Rationality may be universal, but its Western **models** have served colonialism, sexism, racism, and ableism, so Dickman bets on **reforming** it with humility. **Deduction** is valid or invalid (sound when the premises are also true) and gives certainty. **Induction** is strong or weak (cogent when the premises are true) and gives probability through causal analysis, analogy, and generalization. **Abduction** infers the best explanation. **Logical laws are constitutive rules**, and like traffic laws or game rules **they make freedom possible**. Contradictions, self-referential incoherence ("No perspective is correct"), and **fallacies** (formal ones like affirming the consequent; informal ones like begging the question and ad hominem) aren't bad thinking but **non-thinking**. Practicing the rules makes arguments *our own* and makes us *responsible*. Meanwhile the "**think for yourself**" industry, **propositionalism**, Twitter judgment culture, and commodified critical thinking produce a **parody** of thought. Reasoning is a child's incessant "**But why?**", reaching for **the unconditioned** in the **world** (cosmology), the **self** (psychology), and the **totality** (theology). In each domain reason hits **antinomies and impasses**. The classic proofs of God's existence leap from necessity of *thought* to necessity of *things*. Read as Anselm's **prayer** of ultimate concern, though, "God" works as a **predicate** and a **regulative ideal**, expressible only through **symbols**.
