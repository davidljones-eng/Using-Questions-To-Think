## Part III opener: "Make Questions Explicit in Dialogue" (pp. 135–137)

Part III covers two topics: **dialogue** and **questioning**. Dickman's first move is to finish the scaffold he has been building since Part I.

| Domain | Combines and separates… | …into | Perceived as → understood as |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thinking | subjects + predicates | **complete thoughts** | sentence → complete thought |
| Reasoning | premises + conclusions | **arguments** | exposition → argument |
| **Dialogue** | **arguments** | **theses + antitheses** | **conversation → dialogue** |

- **Dialogue is conversation understood. Conversation is dialogue perceived.**
- Dickman separates **polite conversations** from **engaged dialogues**.
- Arguments are complete thoughts combined in a relevant way. **Dialogues are arguments interwoven in a relevant way.**

### Background

- Dialogue has been studied in creative writing and theater criticism, literary theory and narratology, conversation and discourse analysis, and clinical psychology and therapy.
- Many twentieth-century philosophers and theologians followed **Martin Buber** in treating dialogue as the answer to cultural divisiveness and global crises (Peters 1999).
- Liberal religious leaders made **interreligious dialogue** a central platform. They describe an **"axial shift"** (Knitter 2013; Swidler 1990):

```
Premodern:  dictates of RELIGIOUS AUTHORITY
     ↓
Modern:     dictates of AUTONOMOUS REASON and NATURAL SCIENCE
     ↓
Today:      the AGE OF DIALOGUE
```

- This fits the book's critique of propositionalism and adversarial argument. For Dickman, **dialogue is collaborative argumentation.**

### The etymology of "dialogue"

- It looks like ***di-*** ("two," as in divide, diphthong, dioxide) + *-logue* (*logos*), meaning an exchange between two people. **Dickman calls this reading spurious.**
- The prefix is actually ***dia-***, "**through**" or "**across**" (as in diaphragm, diagram, diorama).
- **Dialogue is speaking or thinking something *all the way through*, or speaking *across* a subject matter, so that it reaches its fullest presentation.**

3. **Four orientations** for questioning dialogue, and **responsibility to others** as the counter to domination (Ch. 9)

## The big idea

Dickman stipulates a definition modeled on the earlier ones:

> **A dialogue consists of, at minimum, more than one argument.** Put pithily, "a dialogue is just a pile of arguments."
> **Dialogue (as a verb) is the activity of combining or separating complete arguments.** Its basic particles are **two arguments that differ from each other in a relevant way**, called the **thesis** and the **antithesis**.

```
thinking   integrates  subject   + predicate
reasoning  integrates  premise   + conclusion
dialogue   integrates  thesis    + antithesis   (→ synthesis)
```

The chapter does two things:
1. It identifies features of conversation and dialogue that show their **collaborative** nature.
2. It examines the **dialectic of thesis, antithesis, and synthesis**.

## 1. Good conversation is the clue to dialectical dialogue (pp. 140–146)

### Look-alikes

- Something can look like a complete thought, an argument, or a dialogue **without being one**.
- Understanding a sentence as a complete thought takes questioning. In the same way, understanding a free-floating conversation **as an integrated dialogue** takes questioning (Ch. 8).

### Our intuitions

| What makes a conversation **good** | What makes a conversation **bad** |
|---|---|
| Undivided attention, no judgmental reactions | Condemnation of what you said |
| Feeling **really listened to** | Feeling one-sided |
| Relaxed, laughing together, no filter needed | Being dismissed before you even start |
| "Wow. That was a great conversation." | "We got nowhere." / "That was all over the place." |

### Why "ordinary conversation" is hard to define

- No department studies "ordinary conversation." Should it belong to Communication Studies? Linguistics? Foreign-language programs?
- Literary theory (Fish 1973) and philosophy (Ryle 1953) both appeal to "ordinary conversation" **as though we already understand it**. How can we separate "literary" from "ordinary" language without a clear account of the ordinary? "**Is there nothing worse than being ordinary?**"
- Searle-style attempts tried to build a theory from how speech acts **restrict appropriate responses**. Questions break that model:
  - Some questions ask for **promises**, not assertions. A child asks, "**Can we visit the zoo tomorrow?**" The fitting reply is to make or refuse a promise. Yet we wouldn't call that exchange a conversation.
  - **Changing the topic** doesn't necessarily break the flow. People still feel they're having a conversation.
- Since no single purpose for conversation can be identified, **a complete theory may be impossible.** Some features can still be identified.

### Features of ordinary conversation

**1. It is noninstitutional talk.** Levinson (1983) defines it as talk where "two or more participants freely alternate in speaking… outside specific institutional settings like religious services, law courts, classrooms."

- Institutional forms do exist: witness interrogation, classroom discussion, therapy.
- Ordinary conversation is **extraordinary**, not mundane. It happens **without institutional roles, higher authorities as a safety net, or scripts**.
- It avoids specialized vocabulary, except for terms that emerge within the conversation itself.
- It uses **ordinary language**, which **Perloff** says need not be "literal, denotative, propositional, neutral, referential." Ordinary language can be "**connotative, metaphoric, fantastic**."
- It is messy (Crystal & Davy 1975). People lose their train of thought, make grammatical mistakes, use vague words, interrupt, switch styles, bend rules, and argue illogically and erratically.

**2. Its structure becomes visible in second-language acquisition** (Richards 1980).

- Participants bring **expectations** about how conversations develop and what contributions are appropriate.
- Conversations aren't just chains of **utterances** (minimal functional units of speech). They are **a matrix of utterances and actions bound by a web of reflexive understandings, expectations, and reactions**, set within a culture and form of life.
- **Adjacency pairs** are consecutive utterances where the second is an expected follow-up to the first.
- **Turn-taking** can be **self-selected** or **other-selected**. In a foreign-language setting, you can tell you're being prompted to respond even when you don't understand the words.
- **Conversational repairs** can be **self-initiated** or **other-initiated**. They fix misunderstandings, and they dominate immersion settings.
- **Turn-taking is the crux.** Cues let participants request the floor, give it up, and signal how steady the speaker's audience is (Sacks, Schegloff & Jefferson 1974).

**3. It requires cooperation, not familiarity.**

- Strangers sitting together on a plane can fall into conversation.
- Crystal & Davy's definition: "any stretch of continuous speech between two or more people within audible range of each other who have the **mutual intention to communicate**, and is bounded by the separation of all participants for an extended period."
- **Both people have to be willing**, with a cooperative disposition. Otherwise no conversation is happening.
- **Stenström:** a conversation can succeed despite its irregularities, and **interruptions can be signs of cooperation**.
- Speakers build a **common ground** and a **collaborative floor**, "where the individual speaker becomes far less significant and what is said is jointly accomplished by all speakers" (Wang 2006).

> **Conversations are not dialogues.** Nothing in this empirical account requires a conversation to contain arguments or even questions. A conversation can be nothing but anecdotes, or a game of "**one-upping**" with better jokes and wilder stories.

### Do questions promote cooperation or seize control?

**Stenström:** cooperativeness "is reflected not only in the way questions are answered, but also in the way they are asked." So questioning looks like a key structure of conversation. Others see questions as tools of control:

| Claim against questions | Source |
|---|---|
| Questions are used to **lobby for domination and control**. A questioner can claim a turn, pick the next speaker, and decide what counts as an adequate answer (e.g., yes/no) | Wang 2006 |
| **Closed questions** come across as more dominant than open ones because they limit answer options. The "**chain rule**": one person answers only to hand the floor back to the questioner, which reflects social hierarchy | Fogler 1980 |
| Even open **wh-questions** can carry **implicit criticism**. "**Why did you say that?**" and "**What did you do that for?**" assert something negative, demand an account, and doubt one can be given. "**They dare you to answer, and doubt you can do it.**" | Heritage 2002 |
| **Unwarranted questions** come from someone with no rightful position to ask, such as an interviewer asking about religion or sexual preference, or a student asking a teacher "**Where were you last night?**" They produce "**admittures**": "It's none of your business" leads to the inference "**Whatever it was, it must have been bad!**" | Borge 2007 |

### Dickman's reply

These suspicions **don't apply to genuine questioning.**

- Coercion isn't unique to questions as such. **Genuine questions set constraints that enable creative responses**, the way **six guitar strings** make all sorts of songs possible.
- Questions used for power show up mostly in **institutional discourse**. In hospitals, professionals ask and lay "clients" answer (Kazuko 1999).
- **"And"-prefaced questions** mark routine, agenda-driven "nextness." Picture a parent asking a child, "*And then what did you do? And who did you do that with? And…*" These are **rare in good ordinary conversation**, where both people ask and roles switch, so short question-and-answer packages form naturally.
- In good conversation the questioner wants **new information, not control**.
  - The **"Oh!" change-of-state token** marks discovery and is absent from institutional talk. Picture a lawyer saying "Oh!" after every answer a witness gives.
- In institutions, answers are addressed to **third parties** (a judge or jury). In ordinary talk, the **questioner** receives them.
- **Loaded** questions steer toward an answer. **Genuine** questions leave response options open.
- **Robin Lakoff:** **tag questions**, often described as the most controlling form, occur most often in **women's speech** and frequently signal **deference**, even submissiveness.
- Closed questions often serve as **repair initiators**.

> **Questioning is highly differentiated.** Questions aren't intrinsically power grabs. In good conversation they can reveal **underlying cooperativeness**. Dickman keeps his focus on **genuine questions**, not epistemic imperatives.

## 2. Dialogue has standards of excellence (pp. 146–153)

### Shared experience, and study

- Would you take a trip **completely alone**, with no photos, no notes, and never tell anyone about it? Very few would. **Sharing enhances experience.** ("Remember when you got so scared on the rollercoaster?!")
- **The only way to be sure we're really sharing an experience is to talk about it together.** Talking it over lets us:
  - fit it into the larger storylines of our lives
  - enrich it by emphasizing some features
  - remember it from different perspectives
  - resolve conflicting interpretations
  - **enjoy it again**
- **This is what *studying* something really means.** For **Aristotle**, the heart of happiness is the **life of study**. Happiness is an **activity**, not a feeling ("there are pharmaceuticals and illicit drugs for that").
  - Other enjoyable activities **tire us out**, need **equipment**, or **cost money**.
  - **Study is the most complete and self-sufficient happiness**, "the most enjoyable yet least fatiguing activity" (*Nicomachean Ethics* 1177a). Dickman means the kind of **dialogue that enhances experience**, not cramming for standardized exams.
- **Dialogue about something is this kind of fulfilling study.**

### Dialogue as a game, or practice (MacIntyre)

MacIntyre defines a **practice** as "any coherent and complex form of socially established cooperative human activity through which **goods internal** to that form of activity are realized in the course of trying to achieve those **standards of excellence** which are appropriate to, and partially definitive of, that form of activity."

Every practice has three elements:

```
   ┌──────────┐      ┌──────────┐      ┌──────────────┐
   │   GOAL   │ ◀─── │  VIRTUES │ ◀─── │ ROLE MODELS  │
   │ (internal│      │ (excellent│      │ (exemplars of│
   │   good)  │      │  actions) │      │  excellence) │
   └──────────┘      └──────────┘      └──────────────┘
```

Skills developed in one practice can **transfer** to others.

### (a) The goal: "being at one on a subject matter"

- **External goods** of dialogue are things you could get **without dialogue**:
  - passing the exam (you can study alone)
  - camaraderie (you can enjoy company without dialogue)
  - clarifying concepts (you can use a dictionary)
  - painful self-examination (you can do it alone)
- The **internal good**, available only through dialogue, is "**being at one on a subject matter**." Ch. 8 develops it as the **fusion of horizons**.
- It resembles the **flow state** of play in music, theater, and sports. Games draw us in with a spirit **beyond isolated individuals trying to control things** (Gadamer). We lose ourselves and get "**in the zone**."
- **Good dialogues happen *to* us.** Nobody sits someone down and says, "Now we're going to have a good conversation." We only notice afterward, **with surprise and appreciation**.

**Does dialogue have a purpose?**

- Searle asks what objectives acquaintances have when they bump into each other and start talking. With no specifiable purpose, he concludes, there can be no theory.
- **Gadamer on play:** games have a shared end. "**The mode of being of play is self-presentation.**" Individual moves have purposes **relative to the game**, while the game's purpose refers **only to itself**. What's the point of a **seesaw**? How do you know you're doing it well?
- **The purpose of dialogue is the uninhibited presentation of dialogical movement.**
- In dialogue, "being at one" means "**reaching agreement**" or "**reaching an understanding**."
  - This **doesn't mean believing the same thing.** We can "**agree to disagree**," with a **productive tension** in place of polarizing division.
  - Disagreeing well requires considering the other's perspective, **respecting them as an autonomous thinker**, and staying committed to **productive community** over the subject matter.
  - It **isn't forcing others to submit** to your view.
- **Gadamer:** in true dialogue "each person opens himself to the other, truly accepts his point of view as valid and transposes himself into the other to such an extent that he understands **not the particular individual but what he says**… so that we can be **at one with each other on the subject**."
- **Don't objectify the other by speaking *about* them.** That denies their **discursive autonomy** as a legitimate participant.

### (b) The virtues

Following Aristotle, **virtues help something fulfill its function**, the way **glasses** supply a virtue to eyes. A healthy dialogue needs:

| Virtue | Role in dialogue |
|---|---|
| **Patience** | to take turns |
| **Courage** | to risk making statements |
| **Hospitality and respect** | to share the "virtual field of speech" |
| **Wisdom** | to comprehend the subject matter |
| **Listening** ★ | **the most important dialogical virtue** (Beatty 1999) |

**On listening:**

- **Fiumara:** listening is the neglected, literally **"ob-scene"** (offstage) capacity. It is a resource for criticizing the **hegemonic imperialism of assertiveness**.
- Without listening, assertions couldn't happen. **Can someone make a statement if no one is listening?**
- **Genuine questioning is a form of listening.** It opens a **dehiscent space** in which we come to understand what someone else says. It turns assertive speech into **receptive speech**.

> **"Genuine questions are a way in which we listen with our mouths."**

- **We hear a lot about freedom of speech. Why not the freedom to listen, or the right to be heard?**

### (c) The role models: Socrates

- Every game has heroes. Dickman's example is **Michael Jordan** in the late 1980s. The next generation copied even his quirks, like sticking out his tongue and turning his left foot in slightly when he shot.
- **Who is a hero of dialogue?** If that's hard to answer, why is it harder for something more common than basketball?
- Our word "**academic**" comes from Plato's **Academy**, a **garden** where people enjoyed studying things.
- **Plato's hero of dialogue is Socrates.** He models creative exploration and perseverance in pursuing truth despite discouragement and fatigue, and he talks with all kinds of people:
  - ***Lysis*:** with two wrestlers at the gym, on true friendship (Dickman's favorite)
  - ***Republic*:** with friends after a religious festival, on how to be a person of integrity
  - ***Symposium*:** on love, at a rowdy party

**Two readings of Socrates:**

| The "gadfly" / Socratic Method reading | Dickman's alternative reading |
|---|---|
| A pushy figure who always finds problems. (We love to see heroes fall.) | His real virtue is **insatiable curiosity** |
| The **Socratic Method**: carefully orchestrated questions expose underlying assumptions, show they contradict the original position, and create ***aporia*** (puzzlement, cognitive dissonance) that forces a rethink toward firmer grounding | He **questions everything, even what he himself says**. Incessancy is the basic structure of reason's quest for the unconditioned (Ch. 5) |
| "The unexamined life is not worth living" (*Apology* 38a) | When his partners try to switch roles and question him, they **run out of energy and creativity**, not from fatigue but **because they think they know better**. "**If you assume you know better, you won't have any questions**" (Gadamer) |
| Exposing *aporias* is the goal | Exposing *aporias* is a **by-product** of his real virtue, **attunement with the topic**, which overflows into curious questioning |

- Socrates is "**like an insatiable puppy chasing a ball tossed from who knows where**." He can't help following the subject matter and the twists of the dialogue: "**wherever the winds of dialogue blow**" (*Republic* 394d). That is the flow state of dialogue.

### The spoilsport

> **Spoilsport attitudes undermine dialogue. Listening and questioning enhance it.**

- A **spoilsport** refuses to play along. **The worst kind looks like they're playing but isn't.**
- They **won't take the risk** the game sets up. They **"toy"** with it and protect themselves: "**It doesn't really matter.**"
- **Games are serious.** Children get upset when you use their real name instead of their character's. Fans are devoted to their teams.
- **Watch for subtle topic changes.** **Seesaw example:** you both agree to play seesaw, then as you rise your partner **jumps off** so you slam to the ground. They changed the game into a **prank**. The risk in seesaw is achieving **harmonious balance**, and the spoilsport refuses that risk.
- Some refuse a game because they dislike its rules. Others, "stuck in adolescent attitudes," dislike **any** rules and resent being told what to do.
- **"Some rules do not control you; some rules set you free."** These are **constitutive rules** (Ch. 5). Refusing them means refusing the freedom to play.
- Don't say "**it's *just* a game**." Say "**it's *no less than* a game!**" "**Games are serious business for recreation.**"

### The key risk: openness

- **Not naïve "open-mindedness."** "Are you so open-minded that you are open to being closed-minded?" The point is to be careful about what we mean.
- **The risk of openness in dialogue is the risk of considering that our partner may be saying something *more fitting* than we are.** We stay open to being **wrong** and, if so, to **changing our mind**, which can be painful.
- We take this risk **by asking and sharing questions** (Ch. 8).

## 3. Theses initiate dialogue (pp. 153–155)

### Against the "training-wheel" dialectic

- The engine of dialogue is **dialectics**, simplified as the triad **thesis → antithesis → synthesis**.
- **The naïve version** takes two opposing claims and forms a compromise presented as a superior insight:
  - **Nature vs. nurture** (Samerhoff 2010): "*It's neither just nature nor just nurture, but both!*" Dickman calls this a condescending "Why can't we all just get along?"
  - **Creation vs. Big Bang:** some Jews, Christians, and Muslims say "*Six days might mean billions of years for God.*"
  - The tactic **whittles away the features that make the claims opposites** until only a **superficial common ground** remains.
- **This isn't dialectics.** Dialectical dynamics operate **within each side** (inside nature, inside nurture, inside religion, inside science) on its own terms and in **reciprocity and tension** with the other side. Treating dialectics as merely combining two opposites is **naïve** (Jameson 2009).

### Where the triad came from

- From late German Idealism: **Fichte** presented Kant's deepest ideals (self, world, God) as thesis → antithesis → synthesis. Introductory courses often attribute the scheme to **Hegel**.
- Once you learn it, you see it everywhere:
  - **Marx (as often taught):** bourgeoisie (thesis) → proletariat (antithesis) → communism (synthesis)
  - **Hegel on religion:** "natural religions" such as Daoism and Indigenous American religions (thesis) → "moralistic religions" such as Judaism, Islam, and Confucianism (antithesis) → "**the consummate religion**," which "(surprise, surprise!)" is Christianity alone (synthesis) (Hegel 1988)
  - hydrogen + oxygen → water; sperm + egg → fetus
- **This training-wheel version is simplistic and prone to distortion.** It forces dialectics into **propositionalism**, built out of thesis statements. Still, simplified models can help us build skill with more complex phenomena.

### Theses as *initiatives*

- Argument yields **conclusions** but gives little guidance on **coordinating many conclusions** into wholes. **Symbols**, as concrete regulative ideals, can act as **gravitational centers**.
- What happens when conclusions **conflict**, or when they're **irrelevant** to each other? What about whole **worldviews** in contrast? How does dialogue even start, say between a **Buddhist and a Christian** in interreligious dialogue?
- As **finite, historically conditioned thinkers**, we have to start **wherever we are**, often with beliefs and practices inherited from guardians and communities.
- **Any coincidence can start a dialectical dialogue:**
  - learning about **atomism** leads into **quantum mechanics**
  - mishearing "all of the other reindeer" as "**Olive, the other reindeer**" and wondering why Olive was so mean to Rudolph

> **Theses name the moments of triggering initiative.** The specific thesis statement matters less than what it sets in motion.

## 4. Antitheses move dialogue (pp. 155–158)

### Productive negativity

- The ***anti-*** in "antithesis" names a **negativity that propels** thought, reasoning, and dialogue. It's **productive, not vacuous**.
- "**Constructive criticism**" is **redundant**. All criticism worthy of the name is constructive. Why do we perceive criticism so negatively that we have to reassure people we're not being cruel? **Criticism is not cruelty**, and it isn't negation for its own sake.
- Students see Socrates as a **bully**, as though questioning were intrinsically negative. It is, **if** we wrongly believe "**everyone is entitled to their own opinion**." Socrates questions **so that everyone can realize truths**, not to destroy opponents.
- **Hegel's *Aufhebung*** (determinate negation) "**throws upward**" what is at stake, elucidating it in the light of rigorous thought. Dickman's image: **polishing tarnish off a lamp** to bring out its radiance.
- **Dialectics explain why the *logos* moves like wind.** They make it more than piles of sentences, arguments, and questions. They **integrate parts with a projected whole**.

### Movement: the Eleatics

- Parmenides and **Zeno** posed problems about being and nonbeing. Seen through the triad: being (thesis) / nonbeing (antithesis) → **becoming** (synthesis).

| Zeno's paradox | Setup | Aristotle's reply |
|---|---|---|
| **Achilles and the Tortoise** | The tortoise starts ahead. To catch up, Achilles must first reach where the tortoise was, then where it moved to, and so on forever. Crossing infinitely many intervals is impossible, so motion is an illusion (Curd 2011) | The puzzle **equivocates on "infinity."** Infinite *divisibility* isn't infinite *extension* |
| **The Arrow** | At each discrete moment the arrow occupies one place and doesn't move, so motion is an illusion | **Time isn't made of individual "nows"** like filmstrip frames |

- **Zeno's brilliance is speculative dialectics that provoke and unfold thinking.** What really moves isn't arrows or runners but **our thinking** as it confronts the paradoxes. The irony: while we're being trained to think about movement, **our thinking moves**.
- Questions it raises: *How can there be oneness despite diversity? Why are identity and difference interrelated? What separates integration from mere mixture?*

### Plato: dialectics requires maturity

- Only people with **proven character and intellectual virtue** can use dialectics responsibly, not just to contradict others. Plato imagines an **education system** designed to nurture and test that maturity.
- Dialectics' negativity "**destroys hypotheses**" (mere opinions and mindless conventions) and exposes our intellectual foundations.
- People who aren't ready for that critique may fall into **existential crisis**, or take up a **pseudo-dialectic** and become **contrarians and devil's advocates** who undermine others for the sake of it. Like children who've just learned about contradictions, they get **trigger-happy**. Immature dialectics is "a more sophisticated expression for the basic whine, '**But that's not fair!**'"
- **Productive negativity in Plato:** the "**song**" of dialectics opens horizons of fundamental grounds (the **Forms**). It exposes the "**hypo-**" (under, subordinate) character of **hypo**theses.
- For Plato, what matters is **how the Forms interrelate**, not each one abstracted ("Treeness," "the Beautiful itself"). Isolating them misses his insight (Gadamer).
- **Plato always situates dialectics within the life of dialogue.**

### "Dialectics" (plural), not "the dialectic"

- "**The** dialectic" (or "the new dialectic") **reifies** something embodied.
- **Dialectics appear only on the occasions of specific dialogues.**
- Use the word like **physics, aesthetics, hermeneutics, economics**: a broad field with many instances. Not like singular **rhetoric**, which implies a single "true" or "consummate" rhetoric.

### Hegel's *Aufhebung* in action

| Domain | Determinate negation |
|---|---|
| **Art forms** | **Painting** negates the limits of **architecture and sculpture**, transcending space in ways they can't |
| **Sensation** | Sensation is **frustrated** by sense-data, which isn't discrete enough to satisfy. That frustration prompts **reflection**, and understanding grasps the obscure data in clarified **concepts** |
| **Complete thought** | **The subject is negated and sublimated by the predicate** |

- **Gadamer:** in speaking there's always the possibility of **canceling the objectifying tendency of language**. Hegel cancels the logic of understanding, Heidegger cancels the language of metaphysics, Asian philosophers cancel the diversity of realms of being, and **the poet cancels everything given**. "But **to cancel [*aufheben*] means to take up and use**."
- **The key is movement.** **The whole system of concepts moves.** It isn't individual thinking moving through stable, essentialized concepts.

> Antithesis isn't just an alternative thesis statement. It is what **creates more movement**: the **furthering of dialogue**.

## 5. Syntheses reconcile oppositions (pp. 158–160)

### Editing out Hegel's totality

- Where is dialogue headed? Dickman **edits out Hegel's insistence on an actual moment of totalization**, a final synthesis that would finish dialectics.
- For Hegel, totality seems to have been an inflated **assumption about his own historical position** in the grand dialogue of history, not a mere regulative ideal. "Obviously, life and thought have moved on since Hegel's time."
- **Return to Kant** (Ricoeur): transcendental dialectics always tend toward **metaphysical illusion**. Questions about God, freedom, and the soul can't be settled with certainty. Any non-symbolic answer is an illusion, **not because nothing is there, but because knowledge claims here misapply concepts**.
- So dialectics are **open and ever-renewing**, **oriented by an ideal totality but never completed**.
- **Charitably, even Hegel might agree.** Gadamer reads Hegel as holding that being is never grasped in a single unrestricted intuition (*unus intuitus*) or an infinite Leibnizian monad. All human clarity is clouded by opacity, passing away, and forgetfulness. **Diotima** (*Symposium*) compared human knowing to the life of a **species**, which keeps its being only through the reproduction of individual instances.
- **Our finitude makes us compromise with time.** We get a version of immortality through **reproduction**: having children, and **handing down ideas** in traditions and cultures.

### Growth, infinity, and the spiral temptation

- **Every productive dialogue includes growth.** We learn from each other. Seen across human history, this growth seems to go on **into infinity**.
- **Hegel:** **"bad" or spurious infinity** is a line running past the visible horizon. **"Good" infinity** is a **circle** (Warnke 1987).
- Dickman says this sets up a **false opposition**: line (thesis) vs. circle (antithesis).
- Some will be tempted to answer "**It's both—a spiral!**" That makes the same mistake as treating thesis and antithesis as **standalone propositions**:

```
Life is an infinitely extending line.  (thesis)
No, life is an infinite cycle.         (antithesis)
Actually, life is a radiant spiral.    (synthesis)   ← the trap
```

### What a synthesis really is

> **Theses initiate. Antitheses further. Syntheses paradoxically give a sense of *closure* and a sense of *ever further openness*.**
> **Image: a staircase.** Finishing one step starts the next.
> **A synthesis is a completion not in the sense of an *end*, but in the sense of an *overcoming*.**

The open question, **what role does questioning play in dialectical dialogue?**, leads into Chapter 8.

## Chapter summary in one paragraph

Dialogue combines and separates **arguments**, a thesis and an antithesis, just as reasoning combines complete thoughts and thinking combines subjects and predicates. Empirically, **ordinary conversation** is noninstitutional, messy, turn-taking talk built on **cooperation**, but conversation isn't yet dialogue. Questions in conversation *can* be used for control (closed, chained, "why did you…?", unwarranted questions), mostly in institutional settings. **Genuine questions** are instead constraints that **enable** response, like guitar strings, and they signal cooperation ("Oh!"). Dialogue is a **practice** in MacIntyre's sense. Its **internal good** is **being at one on a subject matter** (reaching understanding, even agreeing to disagree), which comes from the game's self-presenting **flow**. Its **virtues** are patience, courage, hospitality, wisdom, and above all **listening**: "genuine questions are a way we listen with our mouths." Its **hero** is **Socrates**, read as insatiably curious more than as a methodical gadfly. Its villain is the **spoilsport** who won't take the **risk of openness**, the risk that the other person might be more right. Dialectics isn't a training-wheel compromise between opposites. **Theses** are triggering initiatives. **Antitheses** are productive negations (*Aufhebung*) that keep the *logos* moving. **Syntheses**, with Hegel's final totality edited out in favor of Kant's openness, are **overcomings, not endings**, like steps on a staircase.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Dialogue** | Combining and separating arguments (thesis + antithesis). Conversation understood |
| **Conversation** | Dialogue perceived. Noninstitutional, cooperative turn-taking talk |
| ***Dia-logue*** | Speaking *through* or *across* a subject matter |
| **Ordinary language** | Actual communicative language. Can be metaphoric and fantastic |
| **Adjacency pair** | Two consecutive utterances where the second is the expected follow-up |
| **Turn-taking / repair** | Allocating the floor / fixing misunderstandings |
| **Collaborative floor** | Joint accomplishment of what is said |
| **Chain rule** | Answering only to hand the floor back to the questioner |
| **Unwarranted question / admitture** | A question one has no standing to ask / the inference drawn from a refusal to answer |
| **Change-of-state token ("Oh!")** | A marker of discovery, absent from institutional talk |
| **Practice** | MacIntyre: a cooperative activity with internal goods and standards of excellence |
| **Internal vs. external goods** | Goods attainable only through the practice vs. goods attainable otherwise |
| **Being at one on a subject matter** | The internal good of dialogue (→ fusion of horizons) |
| **Play as self-presentation** | Gadamer: a game's purpose refers only to itself |
| **Dialogical virtues** | Patience, courage, hospitality and respect, wisdom, listening |
| **Socratic Method / *aporia*** | Questioning that exposes contradictions and puzzlement |
| **Spoilsport** | Someone who refuses the risk the game sets up |
| **Openness (risk of)** | Considering that the other may be more right |
| **Dialectics** (plural) | The movement of dialogue, appearing only in specific dialogues |
| **Thesis** | A triggering initiative |
| **Antithesis** | Productive negativity that moves dialogue |
| ***Aufhebung*** | Hegel: determinate negation that lifts up, cancels, and preserves |
| **Synthesis** | An overcoming that both closes and opens |
| **Bad vs. good infinity** | Hegel: a line vs. a circle |

## Instructor's suggestions (Appendix, p. 218)

Alongside the ["three things"](../00-Word-Phrase-Sentence-Strategy/README.md) protocol:

1. **Assign "Socratic Dialogue" after the reading.** Students ask a peer, parent, or teammate a question and **try to keep asking questions for up to fifteen minutes**.
   - *Example:* at lunch, a roommate says "This is a good burger." The student asks, "**What is 'good' really?**" When the roommate answers, the student picks a word from the answer and does the same thing: "**What is 'survival' for, really?**" And so on.
   - Students should try to **be Socrates**, insatiable about asking more and more questions.
   - They bring a **200-word report** summarizing the conversation and a brief **reflection**: *What is it like to try to sustain questioning instead of debating or giving your own answers?*
2. **Think, pair, share:** Recall a recent **good** or **bad** conversation. What **three things** made it good or bad? *What seems to be the point of conversations?*
3. **Think, pair, share:** *Do rules restrict us or set us free?*
4. **Examine dialectics**, the movement from thesis to antithesis to synthesis. Perhaps show **8-Bit Philosophy**'s Hegel clip "**Will History End?**" and/or its Marx clip "**What is Marxism?**"

## Discussion questions

- Think of the best conversation you've had. Was it a *dialogue* in Dickman's sense? What was the subject matter you were "at one" on?
- Who is *your* hero of dialogue? What would someone copy from them, the way players copied Jordan's tongue?
- Recall a moment you acted as a spoilsport. What risk were you avoiding?
- Pick a "both/and" compromise you've heard (nature/nurture, science/religion). How would a genuinely dialectical treatment differ?
