## The big idea

Chapter 6 parallels Chapter 3, moved up one level from thinking to reasoning. It covers two topics:

1. **What orientations do questions take in reasoning?** There are four, mirroring the four orientations for complete thoughts.
2. **What happens at the edges of thought and reason?** We reach the "unconditioned," and from there we need **metaphors, symbols, and models**. These build **worldviews**, and they have to stay **open to inquiry and revision**.

The chapter ends Part II with a claim that leads into Part III: **we cannot and do not reason alone.**

## 1. How to question arguments from at least four directions (pp. 117–122)

### Why questioning disappears from reasoning

- We repress questioning partly because **questions don't technically *say* anything**. Faced with an exposition, the questions slip into the background. Dickman points out that the three sentences he just wrote each end in a period, and each answers an implicit question that never gets written out.
- That is why **propositionalism** and **free-thought ideology** are hard to confront.
- Dickman isn't offering an **opposite ideology**, the way a reactionary movement like the Tea Party might set itself against a moderate policy like the Affordable Care Act. His confrontation consists of **exposing propositionalism as pseudo-thinking** and **recovering questioning** from the obliviousness it has fallen into.

### The parallel

| Domain | Questioning coordinates… | …into | Questions open… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Thought** | subjects + predicates | complete thoughts | subject matters → **predicative possibilities** |
| **Reason** | multiple complete thoughts | arguments | premises → **conclusive possibilities** |

### The four orientations in reasoning

```
                              ARGUMENT
          [ PREMISE(S) ] ───── inference ─────▶ [ CONCLUSION ]
                 ▲                  ▲                  ▲
                 │                  │                  │
      (2) Premise-centered  (3) Inference-centered  (1) Conclusion-centered
       "But why?"            "Is it valid? strong?   "And?"
                              fallacious?"

            (4) Rationality-centered: the structure of reason itself,
                and ourselves as autonomous reasoners
```

| # | Orientation | Settled | Open (in abeyance) | Example | "In essence we ask for…" |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Conclusion-centered** | a **premise** | which complete thought counts as *a* (or *the*) conclusion, and what wording fits best for validity or strength | "The car in front of us just put on its brakes! (**And?**)" → "I should step on my brakes now." | **conclusions** |
| 2 | **Premise-centered** | a **conclusion** | which premise or reason leads to it, and what wording gives it the strongest support | "We should probably step back. (**But why?**)" → someone needs to get by (and we want to show the courtesy we'd want ourselves)? Or the bonfire has grown hotter? It's like joining an argument midstream | **premises** |
| 3 | **Inference-centered** | specific complete thoughts | how they relate: are they relevant? deductive or inductive? valid or strong? fallacious? | see below | the **form** of the argument |
| 4 | **Rationality-centered** | — | **the structure of rationality itself**, and ourselves as autonomous reasoners | Is rationality universal, or complicit with Eurocentric hegemony? Should we trust our grasp of reasoning? What constitutive laws make reasoning possible? How does the unconditioned open us to incessant questions? | the **nature of reasoning** |

**Inference-centered examples**

```
P1: If more women are elected US representatives, then the House will be
    more genuinely representative of the US populace.
P2: More women have been elected.
∴   The House is more genuinely representative.

Symbolized:   p ⊃ q        ("⊃" = if, then)
              p
              ∴ q          ("∴" = therefore)        →  modus ponens: VALID, truth-preserving
```

**Malcolm X.** Malcolm X promoted **self-defense classes** for Black men. Racist mainstream media in the 1960s described him as "**promoting hate**" (X 1987). By attacking his intentions and character, they avoided taking the need for self-defense seriously. Was that a valid or strong argument? **Neither.** It was an **ad hominem**, which changes the topic to something irrelevant. Following Chapter 5, it **isn't reasoning in an argument at all**.

**Rationality-centered questioning** is what Part II as a whole has been doing:

- Building a theory of rationality, **even a folk theory**, lets us **know what we're doing**.
- These questions both **generate** arguments and **test** them. We can ask whether an argument is valid or strong, whether its premises are relevant, and even whether it is deployed **only to preserve a dominant class's power**.
- The point of testing: "Will the conclusion **preserve** truth, or will truth **perish** in it?"
- Testing lets us **make arguments our own**: we gain confidence, take **responsibility** and ownership, check for ourselves instead of accepting passively, and **purge ourselves of falsity and nonsense**.

> In all four orientations, we experience arguments **in their mode of being of questionability**. **Questionability is the transition point between an exposition perceived and an argument understood.**

### Comparison with other testing systems

These systems aren't incompatible with Dickman's approach. They grew from the worry about being **duped by nonsense**.

| System | Proponent | Principle | Problems |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Verification principle** | **Vienna Circle**, following early Wittgenstein; **A. J. Ayer** (1952) | Only claims **verifiable by sensation or observed fact** count as complete thoughts. All other claims aren't false but **meaningless**. Nothing invisible or intangible exists | (a) Many claims can't be tested: "**All people act from selfish motives**" absorbs any counterexample (people say **Mother Teresa** helped others to get into heaven). (b) **The principle can't verify itself.** (c) **Scientific laws fail it**, because they apply to the future |
| **Falsification principle** | **Karl Popper** (2002) | Separates science from pseudoscience. A hypothesis is scientific only if it is **in principle falsifiable** by controlled observation or experiment. A theory "works until proven unworkable." Refuted hypotheses get replaced or modified | Works for **general laws**, not particular statements. "**I see a purple hippo when I look in the fridge**" can't be modified after it is falsified in any way that advances knowledge |

**Dickman's diagnosis:**

- The **scientific method is a special case of the general process of questioning** described in Part I.
- Verification and falsification both neglect that testing an argument means examining **at least two conjoined complete thoughts**. They pull conclusions **out of context**.
- **Questioning, not falsification, is the heart of testing.** Questioning depends on context: it only makes sense inside an already-accepted body of concepts, beliefs, practices, and forms of life. (Recall "What year is it?" and "Which direction are we facing? Toward Mecca? Southwest?")
- **Kuhn** calls these frameworks of background assumptions **paradigms** (2012).

### Reflection and the universal

- Questioning in reasoning requires **reflection**, stepping back from immediate pressures so that we can clarify, examine, contextualize, critique, interpret, and synthesize.
- **How is reflection possible?** Dickman says this is probably what "**universal**" originally meant. Experience isn't just a swarm of random particulars. Particulars **accumulate into groups, categories, and relations**.
- **Aristotle's retreating army** (*Posterior Analytics* 100a10–15): one soldier stops and stands, then another, then another, until the army regroups into a new front line. In the same way, our experience accumulates into general categories.

### The bullshit detector

- Reflection lets us check our own thinking and other people's for **indifference to truth** (Frankfurt 2005, *On Bullshit*).
- **Anyone unwilling to follow logical and rational rules in what they assert is bullshitting us.** Poets and comedians can push these boundaries legitimately.
- **Bullshit is worse than lying.** A liar still attends to the truth, even while hiding it. Arguing from indifference is **bad faith** (Sartre).

### Rational critique

> **Critique = analysis, evaluation, purification, and refinement** of thoughts and arguments. It isn't just negative remarks.

Ways to criticize an argument:

1. Show that a **premise is false**.
2. Show that it is **invalid**, so it's only an exposition.
3. Show that the **evidence is weak or distorted**, for example with significant counterexamples.
4. Show that it is **inconsistent** with another argument in the same person's worldview.
5. Show that it leads to **paradox or contradiction**.
6. Show that it is **trivial** or simply repeats its point.

These can look like ways to "demolish opponents." Dickman isn't interested in winning. **All of them are ways to *improve* arguments and to take, or share, responsibility for them.**

## 2. Symbols go beyond the edge of reason (pp. 122–127)

### Human *becomings* and regulative ideals

- A **philosophical anthropology** is an account of what it is to be human. Any account worth hearing has to address our **"effort-to-be"**, what Pamela Sue Anderson calls **"projection" or "yearning."**
- **We are not merely human beings but human becomings.** Our efforts to become are guided by future-directed projections, and those projections are structured by **regulative ideals** (Anderson 1998).
- Ideas of reason such as "the universe" or "god" lie **outside experience and knowledge**, so they can only function **as ideals**. They take the place of transcendent metaphysical entities.
- **Anderson:** regulative ideals "do not constitute knowledge. But they can direct human understanding toward a practical goal **without forgetting the illusion** of claiming to know what is beyond every given experience."
- When regulative ideals work *as* ideals, and aren't mistaken for objectively existing entities, we stay **humble about the limits** of thought. We can't get beyond experience, but we **can change ourselves** by redirecting our energies.

### Constitutive laws vs. regulative ideals (revisited)

| Constitutive laws | Regulative ideals |
|---|---|
| Fixed. They make reasoning possible | **Change and adapt.** They orient reasoning and questioning |
| e.g., non-contradiction, validity | e.g., ultimate grounds and first principles (the unconditioned) |

- Our **time is limited**, so we have to prioritize, asking some questions before others. **What we prioritize embodies our values**, and what we *say* we value can conflict with what our actions *show* we value.
- **Complete thoughts** unify experiences by placing subjects under predicates. **Reason** uses regulative ideals to unify complete thoughts into a **broader systematic structure** (Kant), within the fields of intelligibility of different forms of life (the calendars and directions from Chapter 3).
- Regulative ideals point thinking toward **further inquiry**. We can **project hypotheses beyond rational deadlocks** and hold them as **undecided possibilities**.
- **We have to let go of demanding that others submit to our positions on these matters.** We can't know definitively about the soul, the world as a whole, or gods.
- This use of reason **doesn't increase knowledge, but it improves it**. The improvement is "subjective" in the sense that logic is a function of the I, not in the sense of personal preference.
- It avoids two extremes (Kant):

```
  indolence / apathetic skepticism   ◀──── inspired use of reason ────▶   obsession / fundamentalist fanaticism
```

### The edges of thought

| Edge | Relation to thought |
|---|---|
| **Experience** | **Before** thought. It gives understanding something to predicate about |
| **Action** | **After** thought. Only actions taken according to reason are **free**. An *action* (as opposed to a mere *event*) requires reason, not just psychological motives inside nature's causal chain |
| **The unconditioned** | Can't be thought directly |
| **Contradictions** | A **round square**, a **black/white thing**. You can picture a rounded square, zebra stripes, or gray, but none of those is the thought at issue. **These aren't thoughts to be had.** "Round-square" is perceived words, not an understandable notion |
| **The unavoidable** | Logical laws, self-evident truths, "**properly basic beliefs**". Even these get concretized in cultural and religious symbols |

- **Practical arguments.** In a practical argument the conclusion isn't just a complete thought. It is **a complete thought embodied in action** (MacIntyre). That's why **actions can speak louder than words**, and why comparing what people say with what they do exposes **hypocrisy**. Actions we commit to through reason are actions **we're responsible for** (Aristotle). Dickman approaches **action as text** (Ricoeur 1991).
- **Thinking extends further than picturing.** We can both think and picture a **unicorn**. We can think a **chiliagon** but can't picture it, and we know it has 1,000 angles because we understand the concept, not because we counted them. **Our imagination is more limited than we like to admit.**

### Symbols concretize regulative ideals

- **Regulative ideals are embodied in concrete symbols.** Through symbols we project ideals for **self-realization**, values for **world-changing action**, and unified fields of intelligibility for **worldviews**.
- Symbols and archetypes get configured into **narrative wholes, or myths** (Anderson; Ricoeur, *Time and Narrative*).
- **Tillich:** symbols and myths are context-specific expressions of **unconditioned concern**. Examples: **money** for success, the **flag** for nationalists, the **cross** for Christians.
- **Features of symbols:**
  1. They **can't be invented on purpose**.
  2. They **open up aspects of ourselves and reality** that are unavailable without them.
- **Durkheim's totems** work the same way: a group takes some element of its life, such as an animal, and it comes to represent the community.
- **Symbols and myths open up possibilities for becoming.** Through them we project our yearning and **reappropriate ourselves** for fuller self-realization. This is the basic work of **culture**, the cultivation of fully realized human beings.
- **Zazen example:** sitting in meditation, one **alienates oneself from oneself** by striving to emulate the Buddha, and then **returns to oneself** having sat as the Buddha sat.

### Who controls the symbols?

- Institutions **restrict symbolic production** to the point where many people are marginalized. There's inequitable representation in pop culture (TV) and in **religious imaginaries**, where many assume **Jesus is white** or **God is masculine**.
- **Irigaray:** patriarchal societies have a **marked absence of symbols** for projecting and reappropriating **feminine subjectivity**.
- **Mary Daly's** later work radically tries to create **authentic feminine symbols and myths**.
- **Equality vs. equity (Irigaray):** the quest for *equality* can make **men the symbolic ideal** women strive toward. *Equal to whom?*
- Using "**motherhood**" as the regulative ideal of feminine selfhood can be **complicit with patriarchy**. Irigaray observes that young women want guidance on **becoming women without being subjected exclusively to motherhood, and without being reduced to male identity**.
- **Dickman's diagnosis** (2018c): the cause isn't cultural traditions as such. It's **institutionalized restrictions on cultural production**, where policies decide which symbols get promoted, perhaps according to **market interests and profit**.

### Metaphor carries reason beyond its limits

- We use **analogies to build hypotheses**. Hypothesizing that **minds are unified like objects** opened up the field of **psychology**, and with it self-descriptions like "introverted" and "extroverted."
- **Metaphors literally carry reason beyond the current limits of knowledge.**
- **The limits of knowledge ≠ the limits of thought and reason.** We *experience* events, *think* thoughts, and *know* events and natural laws. We can also **hypothesize alternatives** from openings created by symbolic quests for the unconditioned.
- **Rhetorical-trope metaphor** (replacing one name with an unusual one) ≠ **semantic or sentential metaphor**. The second marks the passage from "the **literal gravel of life**" to its **redemption in poetry**.

> **The poetic redemption of the real:** interpreting experiences by lifting them into discourse and intelligibility, "resurrecting reality differently to new being and new life." **Being is metaphoric** (Ricoeur).

- **Dead metaphors** settle into sediment, and **as metaphors wane, static metaphysics emerges**. We take *argument-as-battle* as what argument **is**, and *time-as-money* as what time **is**. Thought and reason need **revivifying with new meanings**.

The practical use of reason **preserves and expresses freedom of thought** by projecting regulative ideals and unifying within them. Kant used this to develop morality and autonomy. Dickman sets that aside and turns to **worldviews**.

## 3. Regulative ideals build meaningful worlds beyond dogmatism (pp. 127–131)

### The ends of thinking

> One aim of questioning is **thinking**. The ends of thinking are twofold: **actions**, and **worldviews within which actions make sense**.

- **Example:** nonconforming behavior was once explained by **spirit or demon possession**, which belonged to that community's worldview. Today most people turn to **psychology and neuroscience** (personality and neurological disorders). **Worldviews ("social imaginaries") shape how we act, how we treat others, and how we make sense of actions.**
- Reasoning isn't a stroll through an **"argument museum"** with positions hanging on the wall to be observed from a distance.
- **Robert Solomon:** through reflection, articulation, and argument we synthesize "a single, unified, defensible vision… **scattered ideas and arguments are no more [critical thinking] than a handful of unconnected words is a poem**."
- **Worldviews are existentially personal.** They give intelligibility to the "I," which is more than having preferences or biases.

### Concepts: the units of worldviews

- **Predicates and concepts** are the most basic units. They pick things out of the blur of experience and tie experiences together.
- **Concept** = *con-* (together) + *-cept* (to grasp, to tie).

| Kind of concept (Solomon 2010) | Function | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Empirical** | Applies to experience | forward/backward, North/South |
| ***A priori*** | Prior to experience, makes it possible | time, space, number, substance |
| **Existential / teleological** | Orients meaning and purpose | equity, social harmony |
| **Integrating** | Makes whole worldviews hang together | principles, scientific laws, religious destiny |

- **A worldview = an integrated order of concepts, a conceptual framework.** We usually take it as "natural," "just the way things are." We examine it after a **surprising experience**, a **discovery**, or when **someone we respect sees things differently**.

### Multiple worldviews in one life

- People may hold several, even inconsistent, worldviews: **secularism during the workweek, hedonism on Friday and Saturday, Christianity on Sunday**. Can these form a whole, or do we have to choose?
- **Religious studies** often assumes **single religious belonging** as the default and labels multiple belonging "**ambiguity tolerance**" (Coogan 2003). Can someone be **atheist and religiously Jewish**?
- Many people across history see no problem with multiple belonging. Many **Japanese homes** have both a Shinto ***kamidana*** and a Buddhist ***butsudan***.
- If multiple belonging is statistically more common, **perhaps exclusivity is the real cognitive deficit**. Dickman suggests calling it "**false-dichotomy tolerant**."

### Against metaphysical clinging

- Most *a priori*, existential, and integrating concepts are **derived from metaphors and analogies**, now sedimented. **As metaphor wanes, metaphysics rises.**
- **The problem with substance metaphysics:** it treats conceptual frameworks as **how reality must be**.
  - **Thich Nhat Hanh:** this imposition **constructs things to cling to**, and **clinging creates suffering**.
  - **Wittgenstein** (*PI* §599): such thinking is like **tracing the frame of a portrait with your finger**.
  - **Metaphysics takes what is predicative as substantive.** People don't *see* argument *as* fighting. They believe arguing **is** fighting. They believe language **is merely a conduit**.
- Metaphors, analogies, and models reach **dimensions of reality that seem invisible**. They supply cultural coherence, but they **aren't transparent conduits to "reality in itself"** (if there is such a thing).
- That doesn't reduce everything to "mere" social constructs. **Race and sex are social constructs, and they have had powerful, oppressive real effects.**

### Frameworks as useful fictions

- **Models reveal some aspects and hide others.** We should see our frameworks as **fictions**, useful in some respects and **occluding** in others.
- "Fiction" here doesn't mean falsehood. Fictions change lives. There's an **environmentalist organization whose ethic comes from *Harry Potter***.
- **Useful fictions orient us and give explanatory power**, not in the natural-science sense but by **opening up reasons why things go as they do**.
- **A complete thought is (and of course is not) a short story.** Plot integrates characters, actions, and atmosphere. In Joseph Campbell's terms (and *Star Wars*'), **the subject goes on a hero's journey through the trials set by the predicate and comes back greater than it was.**

### Embodiment and the logic of domination

- **Most, perhaps all, metaphors have a physical basis** (Lakoff & Johnson). **Bodily dehiscence** (Merleau-Ponty) makes awareness of awareness possible.
- **Bodies differ**, and that has had two historical consequences:

| Repercussion | Description |
|---|---|
| **1. Varied frameworks** | Different forms of life produce different fields of intelligibility. Which forms of life produced **cardinal** directions (East/West)? Which produced **egocentric** ones (in front of/behind)? |
| **2. Systemic oppression** (more insidious) | Bodies have been **valued differently**. Frameworks are **historically and socially constructed** (Warren 1988), and thinking always comes from a **standpoint**. When metaphors give way to metaphysics, frameworks impose **value hierarchies** ("up" = more valuable) and police a **value dualism**. Warren calls this a "**logic of domination**." Examples: women's bodies treated as "naturally" inferior; hierarchies imposed on the bodies of **people of color in the US** as "only natural" (Lee 2015); a Black man's **self-consciousness in an elevator** with a white person, which people with white privilege don't experience (Yancy 2015) |

### Open-mindedness and humility

- Critical thinking means recognizing **assumptions and prejudices**, **observing observations**, and **evaluating causal explanations** (Warren). It also requires **open-mindedness**.
- **Don't romanticize open-mindedness.** "Are you so open-minded that you are **open to being closed-minded**?" There's a limit. Open-mindedness **can't mean giving every view equal consideration**. It means **recognizing that alternative frameworks, standpoints, fields of intelligibility, and embodiments exist**.
- It requires **healthy humility and healthy skepticism**:

| Skepticism | Purpose | Domain |
|---|---|---|
| **Methodological** | Uses skeptical hypotheses to clarify or gain information. Presupposed by science (Popper: try to falsify even your most cherished hypotheses) | Empirical |
| **Existential** | Doubt about the meaning and purpose of one's life (Tillich) | Existential |
| **Pyrrhonist** (Dickman's favorite) | **Doubt as prayer or meditation**, aimed at tranquility (***ataraxia***). It challenges all dogmatism to **free people from clinging to certainty and belief**. *Skepsis* = to inquire reflectively | Quasi-religious practice |

- All three can be **genuine**. Skepticism is **half-hearted** when someone merely **plays devil's advocate**, which often feels "**canned or instrumental**," not a prayerful interrogation of dogma.

## 4. Instrumental reasoning lacks genuine questions (pp. 132–133)

### Disciplining thought and reason

**Constraints free us.** The laws of music free musicians to play anything from **marches to improvised jazz**.

| What disciplines | What it gives | What it doesn't give |
|---|---|---|
| **Experience disciplines thinking** | *what* to think about | *how* to think about it |
| **Thought disciplines reasoning** | *what* to reason about | *how* to organize frameworks |
| **Reminders that models, analogies, and metaphors aren't metaphysical entities** | guard against overreach | — |

- **Mathematical models and lexical definitions tempt us** to go beyond experience in thinking and beyond thinking in reasoning (Kant).
- Only a **completely different kind of being**, one not bound to subjects and predicates or to inference, could reach a supposed "beyond."

> **The problem with metaphysics: it expresses the wish *not to be what we are*** (Kierkegaard).

### No winning or losing

- **All advances in understanding truth and reality are collaborative.**
- **Disagreeing ≠ refuting.** To refute is to show that **the argument, not the arguer**, fails through invalidity, weak premises, or irrelevant premises. We may also reject an argument for other reasons, such as inadequately justified premises or a concept used incorrectly.
- Because reason's big problems (the soul, divine beings) are **undecidable**, we need the **free expression of ideas** (Kant). Free, reasoned dispute **exposes the illusions** of thinking.
- **There's nothing to fear in disagreement about ideas. We should even *strengthen* other people's arguments.**
- Interactive skepticism and critique don't lead to dogmatic apathy. They **liberate us from illusions** and **undermine dogmatists on every side**.

### Instrumental vs. cooperative reasoning

| **Instrumental / adversarial reasoning** | **Cooperative reasoning** |
|---|---|
| The norm in **institutional and academic philosophy** (Burrow 2010) | The alternative Dickman advocates |
| "Fighting," "sparring," "going for the jugular" | **Mutual gains**, not winning and losing |
| A **value hierarchy and dualism** rooted in win/lose competition | **Mutual respect, consensus, community** |
| "Testing" claims against the **most extreme** opposition or thought experiments | Moves toward **more flourishing of rationality** |
| Reason as **means to an end**, done **alone**, calculating one's own interest | Done **together** |
| **Undermines itself** in competition | — |
| Under patriarchy, women are conditioned to be passive, so their "aggression" stands out more | Women and allies who promote it meet **hostile audiences** "dogmatically addicted" to adversarialism |

### Transition to Part III

> **We need dialogue with others to think and reason. Dialogue is the fundamental dialectic of question and answer.**
> **Plato:** thinking is a conversation with **the otherness within ourselves** (*Theaetetus* 189e–190a).

## Chapter summary in one paragraph

Questioning in reasoning, like questioning in thinking, radiates in **four directions**. **Conclusion-centered** questions ask "And?" of a premise. **Premise-centered** questions ask "But why?" of a conclusion. **Inference-centered** questions ask about form (valid, strong, fallacious, as in the ad hominem used against Malcolm X). **Rationality-centered** questions ask about the nature of reason itself. Each places an argument in its **mode of questionability**, which is where an exposition becomes an understood argument. Ayer's **verification** and Popper's **falsification** principles are special cases that pull conclusions out of context. **Questioning**, which always operates inside Kuhn-style paradigms, is the real heart of testing, and **critique** means refining arguments, not winning. At the edges of reason (experience, action, contradiction, the unconditioned), we rely on **regulative ideals** embodied in **symbols, myths, and metaphors**. These shape us as human *becomings*, but institutions can restrict them, leaving **feminine subjectivity** and others without symbols. Worldviews are integrated frameworks of empirical, *a priori*, existential, and integrating concepts. When their founding metaphors die, they harden into **metaphysics** and **logics of domination**, so they're best held as **useful fictions**, with **open-mindedness** and **Pyrrhonian humility**. Since metaphysics is the wish not to be what we are, and since **instrumental, adversarial reasoning** undermines itself, reasoning has to be **cooperative**. We need **dialogue** to think and reason at all.

## Instructor's suggestions (Appendix, p. 217)

1. Alongside the ["three things"](../00-Word-Phrase-Sentence-Strategy/README.md) protocol:
   - **(a)** Practice the four orientations of questions: **conclusion-centered, premise-centered, inference-centered, and argument-centered** (the appendix's name for what the chapter calls *rationality-centered*).
   - **(b)** Bring up a few arguments to prime students to think about the parts of arguments.
   - **(c)** Select a number of arguments, perhaps at random, to illustrate each orientation.
2. **Think, pair, share:** *How are poetic images (symbols) similar to yet different from concepts? Can we understand a thousand-sided object without picturing it? How do we access the areas of our lives that show us ideals for living?*

### Practice template: four orientations for an argument

Argument: *"The car ahead braked, so I should brake."*

| Orientation | Sample question |
|---|---|
| Conclusion-centered | Given that the car ahead braked, what else follows? Should I also check my mirror? |
| Premise-centered | Why *should* I brake? Is it only because the car ahead did? |
| Inference-centered | Is this deductive or inductive? Is there a hidden premise ("If the car ahead brakes, I risk collision")? |
| Rationality-centered | What kind of reasoning is driving reasoning at all? Is it instinct, rule-following, or inference? Whose model of "rational driving" am I using? |

## Discussion questions

- What symbol functions as a regulative ideal in your life? Could you have *invented* it?
- Do you hold more than one worldview? Where do they rub against each other?
- What dead metaphor ("time is money," "argument is war") shapes your field? What would revive it?
- When were you last "open-minded" in a way that was actually indifference? How is that different from Pyrrhonist humility?
