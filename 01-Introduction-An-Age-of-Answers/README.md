## The big idea

We live in an **age of answers**. Schools, religions, political parties, the media, advertising, search engines, and voice assistants all give us answers, and all of them push **questioning** aside. Dickman says the deeper cause is built into how we picture thinking. He calls it the **propositionalist ideology**: the idea that thought is just abstract propositions, cut off from the questions they answer. The book tries to build a **culture of questioning** in its place, where people become *freer questioners* and so *flourish*.

---

## 1. A "temple of questioning"

Dickman opens with a provocation. Where do people with questions gather? Is there a mosque, temple, zendo, or church for questioners? Could the **question mark** be their symbol of ultimate concern, the way the yin-yang or the crucifix is for other groups? He asks readers to be "evangelists for questions" while working through the book, and he hopes the book can serve as a kind of founding scripture for "the temple of questioning."

## 2. School = leisure: the liberal arts as crafts of free people

- The English **"school"** comes from the Greek ***skole*, "leisure"** (Bourdieu 2000).
- Once a group secured shelter, food, peace, reproduction, and freedom from slavery, it gained **free time**. Dickman notes that historically this mostly meant men.
- People can't do literally nothing, so they filled their leisure with activity. Marks on a wall became **art**. Words rearranged from business contracts became **poetry**. Dissecting an animal just to see its parts became **biology**.
- That is how the **liberal arts and sciences** started: literally, *the crafts of free people*. The term has nothing to do with making students politically liberal.

### Two implications

| Implication | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **1. An unfair hierarchy** | Leisure was a *privilege*. Social-justice calls for accessible, affordable education aim to *spread* this privilege. The deeper reason education is good isn't higher pay. **Education makes you freer.** If you know history you're less likely to repeat it. If you know how to question and argue you're harder to push around. If you know nutrition you're healthier, and health is needed to use free time well. Ideally, schools *reduce* unfair distribution of privilege. |
| **2. The agricultural analogy** | Dickman grew up near Iowa cornfields, pruning and detasseling corn to cultivate it toward ideals. Bonsai and gardens work the same way. Schools **cultivate** students into a specific kind of human being: **a free one**. |

### Eudaimonia: flourishing, not a feeling

- The Greek ideal of ***eudaimonia*** (Aristotle) used to be translated as "happiness."
- Today "happiness" sounds like a *feeling*, something that happens to you passively. So translators now say **"flourishing."**
- Flourishing is something you **do**: the *maximal exercise of our capacities*. Schools should develop the **skills, knowledge, and dispositions** needed to flourish, and those can only be practiced in free time. Practicing them makes people **cultured** (Gadamer).

### School as "re-creation"

- School was supposed to be the ultimate **re-creation**: trying on what it is like to be a different kind of person, experimenting with your freedom, and getting feedback.
- **"Essay"** comes from the French ***essai*, "to try."** Schools are safe places to *try* our capacities for thinking, writing, and acting.
- Compare today's "recreational" drug use and bragging about "blacking out." There, free time is used to **escape** freedom and even the experience of time. You can't experience time while unconscious.

## 3. How school became labor

- Most students don't experience school as leisure. Homework feels like **labor**, and many students "live for the weekend" like the workforce does.
- **Standardized testing** treats students as **containers** to fill with information. Teachers "teach to the test," and students cram, memorize, and recall.
- Calls for **experiential learning** and attention to learning styles. Dickman jokes that people who "can't learn by reading" may be dodging homework. Technically, reading and discussing a book *is* an experience. His real point is that many people agree something is wrong with institutionalized education, from primary school through university.

## 4. The diagnosis: an age of answers

> We live in an age of answers, so much so that we have **neglected questioning**. Questions are **eclipsed** by answers.

**Evidence from schooling**

- Students are graded on *answering* questions, not *asking* them.
- Essays require a **thesis statement**, not a **thesis question**.
- Exams grade answers, not questions.
- Educators say they want students to "question everything," but that is **lip service**. Schools don't put the value into practice.
- Teachers ask far more questions per class than students do. Most of those questions are **procedural** ("When is the essay due?") or check **retention** ("Who won Waterloo?") (Dickman 2009).
- To return to the agricultural metaphor: schools **cultivate answerers, not questioners**. A student without answers gets graded as "low performing."

**Evidence beyond schools**

- **Religious institutions** often treat questions as a sign of doubt or disloyalty.
- **Political parties, popular-science outlets, news media, and advertising** all claim to have the answers.
- The **Industrial Age** has given way to the **Information Age**. Smartphones, Alexa, Siri, and Google make answers nearly instant.
- We link **authority** with having all the answers. Imagine a president asking the press corps a genuinely curious question, such as "What do you think we should do about impeachment?" It seems absurd.

**DRIP: "Data Rich, Information Poor"**

Institutions collect lots of data but can't interpret it to improve themselves. Dickman reframes this. Being "data rich" means having **many answers to questions nobody is asking**. Data becomes relevant *information* only when there's a question it helps answer.

## 5. The deeper cause: the propositionalist ideology

- On this view, thought happens in **propositions** (complete thoughts), and *sentences* in particular languages express them.
- Example: *La nieve es blanca* (Spanish), *Der Schnee ist weiß* (German), and *The snow is white* (English) all express **one proposition**.
- The trouble: studied in an abstract, laboratory setting, propositions seem to stand on their own. But language never happens in the abstract. **Sentences don't sit "in the middle of nowhere"** waiting for us to grasp them. They occur in **linguistic exchanges that include the questions they respond to**.
- Ask: *Who* is asking whether snow is white, and *why* would anyone ask? That question shows what the propositionalist picture leaves out.

## 6. Toward a culture of questioning

- Dickman sets out to build a **culture of questioning** instead of supporting a culture of answerers and its propositionalist ideology.
- Paulo Freire (critical pedagogy) describes **curiosity as "restless questioning"** that is integral to being alive and the source of creativity. It sets us "patiently impatient" before a world we didn't make (Freire 2001, 37–8).
- Goal: through technical skill and precision in questioning, we become **freer questioners** and flourish in more freedom of thought.

### Thought experiments: imagine dwelling with a question

- A politician answers, "That's a good question. I don't know. Let's dwell with it."
- **Google** replies "That's a good question!" and ponders it *with* you.
- A monk or imam gives a speech of open-ended questions without pointing to sutras or hadiths for answers.
- Teachers ask only questions they **don't** know the answer to.

Dickman grants that search engines, teachers, priests, and informed leaders are a good thing to have. Some needs are urgent, and dwelling on the question would be silly or even life-threatening. **But his concern is *thinking*, not answering.**

### The post-truth, technocratic age

- Information is so easy to get and to manipulate that we have moved into a **disinformation / post-truth age of "alternative facts."**
- His concern isn't only anti-intellectualism or nationalist protectionism. Being awash in information **piles answers onto questions nobody asks**. Needs are manufactured so we buy surplus. Answers are everywhere, so **questions start to seem artificial**.
- We live in a **technocratic society**, where means and ends are coordinated *instrumentally*.
- **"20Q"**: an electronic Twenty Questions game whose founder claims it's a living AI that learns and adapts. To Dickman it shows how questioning gets reduced to rote technology. This model also shapes the **mainstream liberal approach to dialogue**.
- **The technocratic paradigm** holds that every problem has a technical solution. Get the smartest people in a room and ask the "right" questions. The real aim is to **do away with questions**, treating them as *problems to be solved*.
- **Gadamer:** "Problems are not real questions that arise of themselves." The nineteenth century gave "the problem" universal validity, a sign that the immediate relation to philosophy's questions had been lost (Gadamer 2013, 385).
- Dickman's project is an ***apocalypsis***, a *revealing*, of the **conditions that make authentic openness possible**: open-ended, creative responsiveness to topics, the world, other people, and other perspectives.

## 7. Overview of the book (the map)

### The scaffold

Dickman builds three domains of cognitive activity as **raised frameworks**. Each one supports the next, and each higher one puts the one below "in a better light."

| Domain | Stipulated definition | Combines / separates | Into |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Thinking** | Combining and separating **subjects and predicates** | subjects + predicates | **complete thoughts** |
| **Reasoning** | Combining and separating **multiple complete thoughts** | premises + conclusions | **arguments** |
| **Dialogue** | Combining and separating **multiple arguments** | theses + antitheses | **ever-unfolding expansions of horizons** |

> In each domain, movement is possible **only via questions**.

Thinking supplies the primitive building blocks for the theories of reason and dialogue. But only after grasping dialogue do we really understand thinking and reasoning. That's why the book can be read in other orders.

> *Note:* the Introduction suggests reading Chapters 1, 4, 6 and then 2, 5, 7. The Appendix for Instructors gives the cleaner pattern **1, 4, 7 → 2, 5, 8 → 3, 6, 9** (see [folder 00](../00-Word-Phrase-Sentence-Strategy/README.md)). You can also read backward from Chapter 9.

### Chapter map

**Part I: Make Questions Explicit for Thinking** ("We should use questions to think")

- **Ch. 1:** Thinking defined by breaking complete thoughts into **subjects** (which identify particular things) and **predicates** (which place those things in an intelligible context).
- **Ch. 2:** How questions relate to complete thoughts, including a **phenomenology of genuine questions** as distinct from commands and imperatives.
- **Ch. 3:** A **logic of question and answer**. Questions pull complete thoughts *apart* while suggesting ways subjects and predicates can be *combined*.

**Part II: Make Questions Explicit for Reasoning** ("We should use questions to reason")

- **Ch. 4:** Reasoning happens in **arguments**, which are relevantly related complete thoughts. **Premises** support; **conclusions** are supported.
- **Ch. 5:** Relevance in reasoning is **logical inference**. **Validity** is distinguished from **strength**. **Rules of inference set thinking free**. Plus a phenomenology of reasoning with questions.
- **Ch. 6:** A **rationality** of how questioning and reasoning work back and forth. Questions pull arguments apart while suggesting how premises and conclusions can be combined.

**Part III: Make Questions Explicit in Dialogue** ("We should use questions to have dialogues")

- **Ch. 7:** **Dialogue** vs. merely pleasant conversation. **Dialectics** is the relevant interaction of multiple arguments. **Thesis / antithesis / synthesis** initiate, move, and conclude dialogue, despite their limits.
- **Ch. 8:** **Questioning is a way of listening.** We share questions to pass complete thoughts to one another. Plus a phenomenology of the **fusion of horizons**.
- **Ch. 9:** The culmination: questioning concerns our very **subjection as beings capable of response**. **Questions constitute us as responsible** and so are a basic embodiment of **human freedom**.

**Conclusion:** A meditation on the **joy of questioning** that comes with flourishing as questioners.

## Part I opener: "Make Questions Explicit for Thinking" (pp. 11–12)

Part I takes up two topics, **thinking** and **questioning**. Everyday puzzles:

- "Don't overthink it." Is overthinking possible?
- "Think before you speak." Can you speak without thinking, or think without speaking, even in an inner voice?
- "I finally relaxed and stopped thinking." **Whose interest is served** by believing that relaxing means pausing thought?

### Theories of thinking across history

| Thinker / tradition | Thinking is… |
|---|---|
| **Aristotle** (ancient Greek) | Identifying which particular **substances** belong in distinctive **categories**, highlighting what many particulars share |
| **Dignāga & Dharmakīrti** (medieval Buddhist) | **Exclusion** of all other phenomena from what one tries to indicate with a name or concept (*apoha*) |
| **Immanuel Kant** (Enlightenment) | **Synthesis** of possible particular sensations with general concepts and categories |
| **Pamela Sue Anderson** (continental feminist) | In part, a process of conceptually explaining and interpreting **metaphorical and mythic images** |
| **Neuroscience** | An electrochemical process of **synaptic networks** |
| **Buddhist meditation** | One of our many temptations to **cling**, which increases suffering because we can't let go |

Part I's plan: (1) the elements of complete thoughts, **subjects and predicates**; (2) how questioning separates and combines them, especially *what it feels like* to question; (3) a schema of **at least four orientations** of questioning; (4) what happens to "thinking" when we **repress or forget** its interplay with questioning.

## Instructor's suggestions (Appendix, pp. 213–214)

Dickman says the best way into the book is to **ask solid, open-ended, genuine questions** using **think, pair, share**:

1. *(To students)* Write a short response: **Would you rather be free or be happy? Why? Do you have to be free to be happy?** Introduce yourself and discuss with students nearby.
2. *(To students)* **Is happiness a feeling or an action, something you do? If it's just a feeling, do we have a pill for it?**
   *(Instructor)* Ask volunteers to share, then riff on their answers, especially in connection with **Aristotle on flourishing**. You might show the **BBC *History of Ideas*** clip on this.
3. *(To students)* **What are some of your favorite questions to think about?** What do you wonder about while doing laundry or showering?
   *(Instructor)* Ask volunteers to share, and riff on them.

## Discussion questions

- Is school, for you, leisure or labor? What would change if it were *re-creation*?
- Think of a recent time you searched for an answer online. What was the *question* behind it, and who else might ask it?
- Can a leader who openly dwells with questions still be trusted as a leader?
- What is lost when a question is treated as a "problem to be solved"?
