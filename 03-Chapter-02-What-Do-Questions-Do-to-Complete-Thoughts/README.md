## The big idea

> **Fundamental axiom: to understand a complete thought is to understand it as an answer to a question** (Dickman 2018).

Without asking the question a sentence answers, the sentence doesn't really "make sense." We *sense* it (we see or hear it), but the complete thought stays buried. Asking its question lets us move from **perceiving** the sentence to **understanding** the complete thought.

Chapter 2 asks **what a question is**. It moves through three layers:

1. **Grammar**: open vs. closed, neutral vs. loaded
2. **Logic and speech-act theory**: are questions just commands? Dickman isolates **genuine questions** that can't be reduced to commands.
3. **Phenomenology**: what is it *like* to hold a genuine question in mind? (its **noetic** and **noematic** poles)

---

## Opening: do we understand questions?

- Asking "What is a question?" already presumes we intuitively know. The aim is to make explicit what is implicit in genuine questioning.
- Questions *facilitate* understanding complete thoughts, so they are **distinct from complete thoughts**.
- In the book's technical sense, **we don't understand questions; we ask them.** When I do understand a question, **I ask it at the same time. Asking it *is* understanding it.**
- (Of course I can fail to grasp a question in Mandarin, or an unfamiliar string of English words.)

### Statements are motivated

- We don't just "make statements." **Statements answer questions.** Grasp and ask the question, and you understand the statement.
- Some performance artists may make incomprehensible "statements" to **avoid taking responsibility** for saying anything.
- **Witness stand:** under interrogation we're unclear about the motive for statements we're made to give, or how they'll be used. A witness who knew the lawyer's motives might even distort their answers.
- **Without the questions that statements answer, the statements are lost on us.** We perceive sentences but don't understand complete thoughts.

## 1. Closed and open questions can be neutral or loaded (pp. 30–33)

### The popular myth

- Advice is everywhere: *Twenty Questions*; interview and polling manuals; therapy literature; first-date advice ("ask open-ended questions"; Aron et al. 1997).
- In education, teachers are told to ask **"higher-order"** questions (Bloom's taxonomy). Synthesis or evaluation supposedly needs synthesis or evaluation *questions* that "demand" more than one-word or rote answers.
- Many specialists **confuse** "higher-order" with **open-ended**, and "lower-order" with **closed**.
- **Dickman:** at the grammatical level, the belief that open questions provoke higher-order thinking is **"mere myth."**

### The grammar of English questions

Interrogatives are marked by one of three things:

1. an **operator (verb) before the subject**
2. an **interrogative word** at the start
3. **rising intonation**

| Type | Form | Examples | Answer set |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open ("wh-") questions** | who, whom, whose, what, which, when, where, how, why; also "if"/"whether" in subclauses | "What is your name?" / formal: "For what did you do that?" | **Unlimited.** Interrogatives work like **algebraic variables** (Harrah 1961) |
| **Closed: yes-no** | operator before subject + rising intonation | "Will the ice cream melt?" | Yes / No |
| ↳ *tag question* | statement + "right?" or repeated operator + pronoun | "The boat has left, hasn't it?" | Yes / No |
| **Closed: alternative** | disjunctive set of 2+ options | "Would you rather be invisible or fly?" / "Which soft drink? Coke, Pepsi, or Sprite?" | **Limited** set |

**Model:**

```
closed questions  ≈  true/false  or  multiple choice
open questions    ≈  fill in the blank  or  short answer
```

Open or closed, a respondent can still answer briefly. Ask a kid "How was your day at school?" and you'll likely get one word.

### All questions are intrinsically neutral

- Questions, like many negatives, are **non-assertions** (Quirk & Greenbaum 1973). **Questions are not assertions; questions are not answers.**
- Test: assertions take **"some-words"** (something, someone). Questions and negations take **"any-words"** (Leech & Svartvik 1975).
  - "She offered her friend **some** chocolates." ✓
  - "She did not offer her friend **some** chocolates." Ambiguous: none, or not all?
  - "She did not offer her friend **any** chocolates." Determinate ✓

### How questions get *loaded* (Piazza 2002)

| Loading device | Biased example | Neutral counterpart / note |
|---|---|---|
| **Assertive words** → expects "yes" | "Has the bus left **already**?" (requests confirmation) | "Has the bus left **yet**?" |
| **Negative bias** → disappointment or annoyance | "**Aren't** you ashamed of yourself?" | Asserts you *ought* to be ashamed |
| **Declarative questions** (assertive traits) | "The guests have had *anything* to eat?!" ✗ | must be "…had **nothing** to eat?!" |

### What matters: neutral vs. loaded, not open vs. closed

- The issue is whether the questioner is **trying to assert something through the question** or **bias the respondent** toward a preferred answer.
- A **neutral** question **respects respondents** and lets them determine answers *for and from themselves*.

> For Dickman, a question is **"open" because it is neutral, not because of how many answers it allows.** Openness means **hospitality**: an open question is *hospitable to a genuine answer or complete thought without distorting it or prompting it with bias.*

- **All questions have presuppositions**, even unbiased ones. **Loaded** questions show a **preference** for an answer or try to **entrap**.
- **Presuppositions** form the **context for any answer whatsoever**, biased or not. "What did you do today?" presupposes you did *something*, but prefers no particular answer.

Some questions really do create moments of responsible, thoughtful engagement. The higher/lower = open/closed binary just can't pick them out. **Neutrality** leads to a better distinction: **genuine** questions.

## 2. Genuine questions are not epistemic imperatives (pp. 33–37)

### Erotetic logic: questions as sets of propositions

- **Erotetic logic** is the formal "logic of question and answer." Many see promise in it for automating **search engines** and information systems such as libraries.
- Questions **contain presupposed propositions**. A question can have a true answer only if its presuppositions are true.
  - "Is it raining?" presupposes "Either it is raining or it is not raining" (Bell 1975).
- False or narrow presuppositions can be **rejected**, or the question can be **expanded** to admit more answers.
  - "Have you stopped beating your dog?" → "I've never had a dog."
- So questions **just are disjunctive sets of propositions**: *"Select one: It is raining. It is not raining."*

### Questions as imperatives

- This overlooks the **performative force** of questions. They also make **requests** about selection, with urgency or indifference.
  - "Did you earn a BA or a BS?" requests a choice between two and disallows "neither," though "neither" can *reject* the question.
- So many theorists class interrogatives as a **species of imperative**: **epistemic imperatives** (Åqvist 1965).
- Standard case: (a) the questioner doesn't know the answer and (b) expresses what they do know. Form: **"Make it the case that I know X."**
  - "Which US presidents were generals?" → "For each X where X is a President and a general, 'make me know' that X was a President and a general" (Harrah 1982).
- **Classroom or exam version:** "Make me know that you know X." Teachers don't ask exam questions they can't answer.

### Speech-act theory: questions as directives (Searle)

- Questions **perform** like imperatives. They request speech acts whose proper form the question has already prescribed.
- "Where are you from?" is a polite imperative: "**Tell me** where you are from."
- "What is your name?" → "**Tell me your name.**" → "Select one: Your name is Muhammad. Your name is Ruth. Your name is … [ad infinitum]."
- Being translatable into a command doesn't make a question **insincere**. Searle's **felicity / sincerity conditions** (1969): the questioner (1) doesn't know the answer, (2) wants to know, and (3) uses the utterance to try to get it.
- **Most everyday questions are *sincere*,** whether phrased as a question or as a command.

### The worry: are questions tools of oppression?

- Are there **any** questions that can't be reduced to commands? Why have questions at all if soft imperatives do the job?
- **Rebecca Comay** imagines a history of the "institutionalized violence lurking behind the apparent guilelessness of the question":

| Domain | Questioning as power |
|---|---|
| Juridical | investigation, interrogation, cross-examination |
| Pedagogical | *disputatio*, quiz, exam |
| Religious | inquisition, catechism |
| Medical | examination, inquest |
| Surveillance | interview, questionnaire |

- **Žižek's** totalitarian: "*It is we who will ask the questions here!*" (see also Fiumara).
- If all questions are disguised commands, maybe we should **give up questioning** for the sake of less oppressive social structures. Some clinicians following **Carl Rogers** refuse to ask clients any questions at all.

### Dickman's wager: genuine questions

> There is a **distinctive kind of questioning that cannot be reduced to commands**. Dickman calls these **genuine questions**, as distinct from merely *sincere* ones, and makes them the **paradigm** for analyzing all questions (instead of epistemic imperatives).

| | **Sincere question / epistemic imperative** | **Genuine question** |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Needed answers and information | **Not primarily** answers |
| Driver | **Deprivation-driven**, calculative | **Surplus-driven**, a desire of one who "lacks nothing," oriented by "the order of the Good" (Levinas) |
| Words treated as | Signs for processing information (like dictionary entries) | Words with meaning in complete thoughts |
| Replaceable by | A command, or a **nametag** (just look and see the name) | Nothing. Irreducible |
| Outcome | An answer | Often **more questions**, and **shared asking**: getting another person to ask *with* us |
| Social form | Transaction | ***Communitas*** |

- **Levinas:** if getting information were the only function of questioning, we'd have to wonder whether we're capable of complete thoughts in which words have meaning at all.
- **We can question for the joy of it,** dwelling with what questioning opens up, without feeling deprived of answers.
- **It is naïve to subordinate questions to answers.**

## 3. A phenomenology of genuine questions (pp. 37–43)

### What phenomenology does

- It focuses exclusively on **consciousness**, on *what it's like*.
- Step one: **bracket** (*epoché*) our normal, natural assumptions. Here, that means bracketing the assumption that **questions exist for the sake of answers**.
- **Analogy:** suspending disbelief at a sci-fi or fantasy movie. Without it you can't enjoy the film.
- Goal: isolate what it's like to be **conscious of questioning** while considering a question. Treat questioning as **something very weird**, worth exploring for its own sake.

### Recurring pairs in the literature (genuine questioning sits on the right)

| Derivative | Genuine |
|---|---|
| passive | **active** (Morgan & Saxton 2006) |
| guiding | **grounding** (Blok 2015) |
| derived | **originary** (Meyer 1995) |
| naïve | **phenomenological** (Plotka 2012) |

### Not an isolated ego introspecting

- **Ricoeur:** consciousness is "outside itself," *toward meaning*, before meaning is *for* it as an ego.
- The ego has **no mastery** over complete thoughts, understood or intended. They shape self-consciousness *before* the ego "means" anything (Gadamer).
- We don't become self-conscious egos *first* and then speak. **We learn languages and models of ourselves, and only then form the idea of a separate, intending ego.**
- So genuine questioning isn't modeled on the **subject/object duality** of sensation (ego here, thing there).
- **Images:** we treat images as weak copies of objects, but **poetic images reverberate because of complete thoughts understood**. *We "see" some things by first reading or hearing them* (Ricoeur 1979).

### Two poles

| Pole | Ordinary expression | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Noetic** (the *acting*) | "*asking* a question" | The activity of questioning. Merleau-Ponty: questioning is the **essence and origin of all noetic activity** |
| **Noematic** (the *content*) | "the question *asked*" | What one is conscious of while questioning: the *questioned as questioned* under the *epoché* |

#### The noetic pole: asking

1. **Unreflective participation, not introspection.** Like music or games, **we can get "in the zone"** with questioning.
2. **Questioning is openness**, consciousness's original aiming at things. Merleau-Ponty calls it our **"ontological organ"** and "ultimate relation to Being."
3. **Contraction / dehiscence.** Questioning is more than perceptual aim. It includes a *contraction* to consider possible answers, like Merleau-Ponty's **dehiscence**: the body splitting into *feeling* and *being felt* (touching your own hands). We don't receive "films" of things (ancient *effluvia*). We **envelop** things with our dehiscent bodies, which is why experience comes "**in person**" or "**in the flesh**."
   - **Irigaray:** don't abstract questioning from "its carnal taking root."
   - The body as the **"I can"**: a network of potentials oriented by **anticipatory projections, or prejudices**, which come from conformity with inherited norms and culture (Gadamer; Ahmed 2006).
4. **Questioning suspends.** It has the logical structure of **suspending complete thoughts and judgments, including prejudices**. It keeps a **distance** from them while staying **open** to them as possible answers (Gadamer; de Beauvoir). It **doesn't eradicate** embodied prejudices. It **puts them at risk** by testing them as possible answers.
   - **Gadamer:** genuinely speaking one's mind "risks our prejudices—it exposes oneself to one's own doubt as well as to the rejoinder of the other."

> **"Questioning is the skin of understanding and answering is the flesh of meaning."** Genuine questions are where understanding "touches" itself.

5. **Need vs. desire.**
   - Heidegger: every questioning is a **seeking**. Searle: **wanting to know** is a sincerity condition.
   - **Levinas** disagrees: if information is all we need, we don't need to *ask*. The **request and prayer** inside a question attest to a **relation to the other person**, and that is the question's *originary* modality.
   - **Anxious grasping for information distorts** exploring and dwelling with others. A want for knowledge may *motivate* questioning but **need not accompany it**.
   - In genuine questioning **we don't know where we'll end up**. There's no "final solution" (Meyer). Like **Socrates**, we follow thought "wherever it, like the wind, blows."
   - Genuine questioning is a **surplus-driven, shared desire**, an **opportune responsibility**, a **letting go to the subject matter** (Irigaray). *It is how we get in the zone.*

#### The noematic pole: the question asked

- The content of questioning is **not the possible answers**, contrary to erotetic logic's focus on presupposed propositions. It is **the question asked**.
- **Ricoeur's distinction:**
  - **Reference**: the *what* the question's sentential subject seems to point to
  - **Sense**: the articulated interrogative sentence, *subject + proposed predicate*
- **Answers** synthesize the **identifying** function (subjects) and the **elucidatory** function (predicates).
- **The hegemony of reference:** we reduce "the meaning" to what the subject points at (Wittgenstein's *Bedeutung*). **Irigaray:** this **freezes the flow of lived experience** and forces moments to serve our needs.
- **References are not answers**, because they aren't complete sentences.
- **Meyer:** the subject of an answer refers back **not to an external object but to the interrogative pronoun** in the question. **Answers "refer" us to their questions**, not to objects "out there."

### Sense, understanding, and its limits

- **Sense displaces reference.** It moves us from direct experience to **interpreting and speaking about** it. **To discuss experience is to dignify it** by raising it into understanding.
- Building a complete thought doesn't guarantee understanding it. We can see or hear a sentence without grasping it. **Genuine questioning makes it possible to *receive* sense** (Ricoeur), letting sentences appear as complete thoughts.
- **Gadamer:** "**Being that can be understood is language.**" Not sentences perceived, but sentences *understood*.

> **Only sentences can be understood.** We understand **discourse**, no more and no less.

- **Talal Asad on death and suicide bombings:** "in any death there is nothing to understand," and chance deaths are more horrible because no comforting story can redeem them.
- **Dickman's reframing:** this despair isn't a failure of understanding. It comes from **inflated expectations** that "smear" understanding across everything. Events, experiences, and people **aren't complete thoughts**, so they aren't objects of understanding. They *provoke us to speak*, and they make us poetically productive.
- Dickman focuses on the **meaning-*receiving* subject**, the one who opens body and consciousness to receive meanings by asking questions. Asad's focus is the meaning-*making* subject.

### We understand what people *say*, not *people*

- **Gadamer:** in true dialogue each person opens to the other "to such an extent that he understands **not the particular individual but what he says**." Therapy or criminal interrogation, which focus on the individual, aren't really situations of reaching understanding.
- **Empathy ≠ understanding.** We may *empathize* with others, but **understanding is of complete thoughts**.
- **"You will never understand someone else's experience."** This sounds profound and protective. It guards against **assimilating** others into the "same," and it may react against a wish for telepathy.
- Dickman treats it as a **grammatical rule**, like "everything" not being a subject. **People aren't complete thoughts**, so of course we don't "understand" them. That isn't disappointing. It's a **category mistake** to expect it (Thich Nhat Hanh; Wittgenstein).
- The distinction between the **order of understanding** and the **order of empathetic experience** does two things:
  - **protects others from naïve assimilation**
  - **protects understanding from pretentious inflation**

> "It is not that I understand you, but rather what you have to say in response to questions that are actually being asked."

### Transition

What, then, is the **sense of genuine questioning**? So far only negatives: not reference, not events, not other people. Chapter 3 gives the positive account, **a different logic of question and answer**.

## Chapter summary in one paragraph

To understand a complete thought is to understand it **as an answer to a question**. We don't understand questions. We *ask* them, and asking is understanding them. Grammatically, the open/closed distinction (wh- vs. yes-no or alternative) doesn't track "higher-order thinking." What matters is whether a question is **neutral or loaded**, and real openness means **hospitality** to the respondent's answer. Logicians and speech-act theorists treat questions as **epistemic imperatives** ("make me know X"), and critics like Comay and Žižek see questions as tools of oppression. Dickman holds that **genuine questions** can't be reduced to commands: they're **surplus-driven**, not need-driven, and aim at **shared asking** (*communitas*) more than at answers. Phenomenologically, genuine questioning is an embodied, unreflective **openness** that **suspends and risks our prejudices** ("the skin of understanding"). Its content is the **question asked**, whose answers refer back to the question, not to external objects. Only **discourse** can be understood. We understand **what people say**, not people, and this keeps empathy and understanding properly distinct.

## Instructor's suggestions (Appendix, p. 215)

Alongside the ["three things"](../00-Word-Phrase-Sentence-Strategy/README.md) protocol:

1. **General warm-ups on questions.** Song titles that are questions ("Are You Gonna Go My Way?", "Who Can It Be Now?"). The **"36 Questions That Lead to Love"** (Aron et al. 1997), e.g., "What constitutes a 'perfect day' for you?" and "What do you value most in a friendship?"
2. **Translate informational questions into commands:** "What is your name?" → "Tell me your name." An **exam** works well, since its prompts are questions the teacher already knows the answers to. Use the examples to **pull out assumptions**, e.g., "This person has a name."
3. **Get comfortable with phenomenological suspension.** Discuss what it's like to get in the mood for a fantasy movie or novel. How can you enjoy it when you know the plot isn't real?
4. **Think, pair, share:** *Can you have empathy without understanding (in the book's technical sense), and understanding without empathy?*

## Discussion questions

- Rewrite three questions you were asked today as commands. Which ones *resist* translation?
- Find a loaded question in a news headline. What does it assert? Rewrite it neutrally.
- When was the last time you asked a question you didn't need answered? What did it do?
- Do you agree that "you'll never understand my experience" is a category mistake? What's gained and lost by putting it that way?
