## The big idea

Dickman **stipulates** a working definition so the rest of the book has a shared topic:

> **Thinking is the activity of combining (or separating) subjects and predicates into complete thoughts.**

A **complete thought** is a **subject + predicate** (noun + verb), as in elementary grammar. The *Schoolhouse Rock* song puts it: "Mr. Morton is the subject of our sentence, and what the predicate says, he does."

```
           COMPLETE THOUGHT
   ┌──────────────┬────────┬──────────────────────────┐
   │   SUBJECT    │ COPULA │        PREDICATE         │
   │ "Your keys"  │ "are"  │ "on the kitchen counter" │
   └──────────────┴────────┴──────────────────────────┘
   answers:              answers:
   "What is being        "What is being said about
    talked about?"        the subject?"
```

The chapter covers (1) subjects, (2) predicates, (3) how they combine (the copula), (4) how language and thought relate, and (5) who does the thinking and what makes it *critical*.

## 1. Sentential subjects pick out particular things (pp. 14–16)

**What a subject is.** Proper names, pronouns, definite descriptions, indexicals, and similar terms. A subject **picks out some singular thing** as the topic and answers **"What is being talked about?"**

**How it works.** Amid the flow of sensations, some sliver of experience **stands out** from the background, which Dickman calls the **horizon of experience**. Once it stands out, we **objectify** it (make it an object) so a name or pronoun can point to it.

### Objectifying people, and reification

- Applied to **persons**, objectification is wrong. People aren't things or tools to use and throw away.
- Yet to *talk about* people we have to **"subjectify"** them, isolating them as sentential subjects against a wider horizon. So we should be careful how we do it.
- **Reification** means treating something abstract, like "love" in "Love is blind," as a concrete object. It happens all the time and is **fine, unless we forget or misrecognize that we're doing it** (Bell 2009). Then we take our *heuristic* for how things really are, and our way of *representing* things for how things *must* be.

### Inverting the "problem of reference"

- The **problem of reference** (Nye 1998) assumes a rigid split between *thought and language* (inside minds or social conventions) and *reality* (outside). The worry is how our arbitrary words could ever cross that gap.
- Dickman diagnoses this as a **name-centered** view of language, with words as **nametags or Post-it labels** stuck on objects. It asks whether mental representations "correspond" to things "out there."
- **His alternative:** we don't need to figure out how language corresponds to reality, because we aren't assuming the representational model. **Experiences, especially the ones worth the name, stand out and compel us to speak up.**

### A limit: things that can't really be subjects

> "Everything just increased by ten times its size."

- It *looks* complete: subject "everything," predicate "increased by ten times its size."
- But a subject must pick out **one thing in contrast to something else**. "Everything" is the totality, *including* all horizons, so **nothing is left to contrast it with**. It can't work as a sentential subject.
- By contrast, "Mandy and Xavier just increased by ten times their size" works.
- The apparent "deep thought" (shower-thoughts material, or a metaphysical puzzle) **dissolves** once we notice this surface-grammar problem.
- The claim "'everything' can't be a sentential subject" is about a *concept*, not about reality as a whole.
- Other candidates to test: **"nothing," "being itself," "the universe."**
- This doesn't mean we should never try to think about such things. Trying is excellent exercise for thinking. **Nāgārjuna** used such terms to expose the limits of thought and to help someone realize nirvana.

## 2. Predicates situate sentential subjects (pp. 16–20)

**Predicates are what we say about a subject.** Thought and language *really get started* with predication, the **distinctive trait** of complete thoughts.

### Signs vs. words

- A dictionary entry is only a **virtual or potential** instance of language.
- Language gets **actuality** and "traction between us" only in **predicative use** in a specific moment.
- **"There are no 'words' in dictionaries, only abstract signs."** Words *decompose* into signs when they're put in dictionaries.
- **Ricoeur:** dictionary signs become words "charged with expression and meaning" only when used in a sentence (Ricoeur 1998, 34–5).
- **Language-learning example:** while you still translate term by term, you aren't using the new language's words *as words*. Fluency means you can predicate without sign-by-sign translation. (Some say you're fluent once you dream in the language.)

### What predicates do

- Predicates answer **"What is being said about the subject being talked about?"**
- Subjects tempt us to picture the world as **piles of discrete things**. Predicates show **relations and processes**.
- **Predicates place subjects in a *predicament*.** Subjects are *predicable*, that of which one can predicate (Kant).
- **Keys example:** "Your keys are on the kitchen counter." Being on the counter is the keys' predicament. Maybe the most relevant part is "*kitchen*": the keys are inside, behind the door you just locked. A thought can show us our *own* situation too.
- **Predicates situate subjects in an articulate, intelligible context.**

### Don't treat predicates as bigger names

- Treating predicates, or whole complete thoughts, as just **bigger names** reduces predication to identification.
- Taking naming as the essence of language is "a leftover from sorcery, the residue of 'the magical theory of words'" (Ricoeur 2003, 89).
- *Predication* is the Latin rendering of the Greek word for ***category***. **Predicates are categories, and categories are predicates.**
- People resist being categorized. **Spaemann:** persons are *nonidentical* with their predicates. Yet categories are crucial in **zoology** (genus and species) and **movie genres** (horror fan, not stuck at a rom-com).
- Taxonomies tempt us into **Platonism**: abstract phrases seem to name ideal entities in some mind-independent realm, as if "love itself" were a thing, or as if rom-com love were *true* love. But can we point at "human being" apart from a particular human, or sense "treeness" apart from a specific tree?

**Dickman's reversal:** Don't fold *predicate* into our modern idea of *category* or *label*. Rethink *category* and *label* through a renewed idea of *predicate*.

> **Predication is about showing and revealing relational networks about a subject by placing it in a relevant context.** Predicates *supersede* subjects (Gadamer; Dickman 2016). They play an **elucidatory** role, raising subjects into an intelligible, meaningful order.

**Direction example.** One direction stands out as a subject. In one order of meaning it's "facing North." In another it's "facing toward **Mecca**." The second predicate reveals a relevant aspect and context.

### Perpendicular, not parallel

| Inherited "picture" model | Dickman's predicative model |
|---|---|
| Complete thoughts are **pictures of facts** (early Wittgenstein 1922) | Predicates **lift** subjects into a "higher" order and **raise** them into the "light" of complete thoughts |
| Thought and language run **parallel** to the world | Thought and language run **perpendicular** to the world |
| Privileges the **subject** side: complete thoughts as big names | Privileges **predication**, with no "redundant layer" |
| Produces the **problem of reference** | Thought and language **animate** the world, turning chaos into intelligibility (compare "Let there be light" in *Bereshit*/Genesis) |

- **Fields of intelligibility** are made up of **forms of life, communities of practice, and narrative traditions**.
- **Because of predication we live in a *world*, not merely survive in an *environment*** (Ricoeur 1976).

### Examples: titles are predicates, not names

- **Siddhartha Gautama** is the name. **"Buddha"** ("awakened one," from the Sanskrit root *bodh*, "awake") is a *title* predicated of him. A community seeking an awakened one was in effect asking, **"Who will be my Buddha?"** In Gautama they found their answer.
- The same goes for **Muhammad** ("Prophet") and **Jesus** ("the Christ"). These are operators within fields of intelligibility local to particular communities. Not every community has these concepts, and not every community that has them applies them to these figures (Dickman 2017).
- **Sports:** player #23 "is the forward." The word works in both soccer and basketball, and each game is its own field of intelligibility, so you need context.

## 3. The copula is more than the sum of its parts (pp. 20–22)

- **Copula** names the **basic unity or connection** that links a predicate to a subject. It's usually a form of **"to be."**
- It's **implicit** even when "to be" doesn't appear, because **being is implied in every act of thought**.
- You can't think **nonbeing / nothing** as a subject, since there's nothing to isolate. **Being makes every thought possible.**
- "Being" is *not* a label for a mind-independent reality. Minds and social constructs are part of being too. Here, **"being" is the semantic or intelligible link between subject and predicate**, and **semantics and being are practically coextensive**.
- **Failed combination:** "A bachelor is a married man" is false partly because subject and predicate **don't combine into a being, a meaning**.

### Combination is productive

- Synthesizing subject and predicate yields a **new entity**, the complete thought, which is a **different species** from the signs it's made of.
- **Predicates subsume or absorb subjects.**
- The *copulation* metaphor fits: subjects and predicates **copulate to give birth** to complete thoughts, or meanings. They aren't "just a pile of signs."

### Nothing above the complete thought

- There is **no higher species**: complete thoughts can't be *synthesized* with each other into a new entity (Dickman 2014).
- They can only be **added**, by:
  - **Concatenation**: collecting complete thoughts into a series (an argument, essay, or narrative)
  - **Interpolation**
- These are interactive *relations*, not syntheses. Genres supply the conventions for such compositions.
- So the **complete thought is the fundamental unit of language and thought**. Signs and symbols, as *elements* of sentences, derive from their proper home in complete thoughts.

```
signs ──(predication / copula)──▶ COMPLETE THOUGHT ──(concatenation)──▶ arguments, essays, narratives
      synthesis: a new being                         relation, not synthesis
```

## 4. An intersection of language and thought (pp. 22–23)

**The puzzle:** Can we think without language? If languages are arbitrary and language is crucial to thought, is all thought arbitrary?

- Language and thought **aren't identical**: we can **say** what we can't **think** ("Everything just increased by ten times its size").
- Language **extends further** than complete thoughts. We can build more expressions than we can understand, including nonsensical (unthinkable) ones.
- Yet **poetry, metaphor, and creative discourse push the frontier of the thinkable** outward. Metaphors reveal new resemblances that old classifications hid and "establish new logical frontiers on the ruins of their forerunners" (Ricoeur 2003, 233).

### Dickman's framework: two sides of one coin

| | Dimension | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Sentences** | **Sensibility** | Complete thoughts *as perceived by the senses*: written or signed (seen or touched), spoken (heard) |
| **Complete thoughts** | **Intelligibility** | Sentences *understood* |

- **Familiar experience:** you "read" a page and reach the bottom wondering what you just read. You saw sentences in your own language but grasped **no complete thought**. (He returns to this in Chapter 2.)

**Consequences**

1. **Thinking is intrinsically verbal.**
2. **We can always say more than we understand.**
3. Complete thoughts need **nouns and verbs**, subjects and predicates.
4. Words outside sentences are no longer words but **discrete signs**. That's why **semiotics** (the study of signs) differs from **semantics** (the study of meanings). We can manipulate signs without understanding them.
5. **Complete thoughts are not representational.** They aren't worded pictures of facts. **Predicates render intelligible the parts of experience that sentential subjects set in relief.**

## 5. Orientations for critical thinking (pp. 23–27)

Two more questions: **Who or what is the agent of thinking?** And **where or when does a thought happen**, including the structures that inhibit or oppress thought?

### The ambiguity of "subject"

- *Sentential* subject vs. **subjectivity**, the "I."
- **Descartes:** the I is a *thinking thing*.
- Is thinking something the I **does**, or something that **happens to** it? We *choose* what to think about, yet we also say "that **struck** me" and "it **occurred** to me."
- **Buddhist "monkey mind"** (Suzuki): thought swings from branch to branch. Meditators don't try to control it. They step back and **observe**. But who steps back, and who leaps? Should we identify with the **leaper** or the **watcher**?
- These questions matter in an age worried about **free thought and free speech**. If there's no agent of thinking, who is free?

### Responsibility over originality

Dickman sets these questions aside and **de-emphasizes the worry about being original**, about being the originating cause of thoughts.

> We should **take responsibility for thinking** (combining and separating subjects and predicates) **as if it were our own, even if we are not the originator.** The aim is to be **careful with the thoughts we do think**, whether or not we started them.

This becomes more significant once questions enter the picture (Chapters 2–3 and 9).

### The ego as a reification

- The ego tries to set itself up as a **self-sufficient ultimate origin** (Ricoeur 1975).
- But the ego is **transcendent to consciousness** (Sartre 1960). It's a **reification** of ourselves when we reflect on ourselves, squeezed into the "box" of sentential subjects just as "love" is.
- **Subjectivity is not an object**, so it **systematically eludes consciousness**, which is always conscious *of* some thing.
- **Self-consciousness:** a nervous speaker has built a model of themselves as a "bad public speaker." Do we likewise build a model of ourselves as "thinking things"?
- **"Thinking," at the moment we think something, can't work as either the subject or the predicate of a complete thought.**

> **Complete thoughts do not belong to us as egos; we belong to them.**

- Before we build an ego through reflection, we **already participate** in families, societies, and states.
- **Gadamer:** "The focus on [egos] is a distorting mirror. The self-awareness of the individual is only a flickering in the closed circuits of historical life" (2013, 289).

### Participation in thought: from the divine mind to subject matters

- **Aristotle, Ibn Sina, and Spinoza:** thinking goes on in the **"divine mind"**. When humans think, they **tap into a stream** that started long before us and continues long after. "The god thinks, and we just happen to embody aspects of that god's thought."
- **Dickman's modification:** keep the idea of *participation* but drop the divine *ego* or agent. Look instead to **subject matters themselves**:

> Subject matters **radiate predicative possibilities**, a **horizon of possible meanings** that is always in flux and makes any particular thought possible (Dickman 2016).

- When a predicate fits, it feels as if it was **always there** for that subject.
- **Metaphor:** "**Paul is a lion.**" The literal predication *implodes* so the metaphorical one can emerge from the ruins. If it reveals Paul's essence and vitality, we may be surprised we never thought it before.
- **Who thinks, us or the subject matters?** Dickman deliberately leaves the question **suspended**.

### What makes thinking *critical*?

- Neither combining nor separating is critical in itself.
- **Separation** can be shown with "not": "Your keys are **not** on the kitchen counter." This *separates* the predicate from the subject. It isn't a combination with a "negative predicate"; that objection comes from equating language and thought too closely.

> **Thinking becomes critical when it is reflexive or metacognitive: we think a thought and, at the same time, think about our thinking of it.**

- All criticism **puts thoughts and the thinking of them into a context**, and that context **reveals their limits**.
- **Standpoint epistemology** (Collins 1990; Lugones & Spelman 1983): there's no neutral or universal view. A view is one embodied location among many.
- **Other reflexive avenues:**
  - psychological motivations for a thought
  - economic conditions that make it possible
  - racist presuppositions in it (DiAngelo 2011)
  - transcendental conditions of any thought at all (Kant)
- **Most academic disciplines are fields for criticism.**

> **Critical thinking is self-criticism.** It applies the basic principles of disciplines to a specific instance of one's own thinking.
> **The mark of excellence in critical thinking is comprehensiveness in interdisciplinarity.**

- Expertise in one field (for example, neurological predispositions toward bias) allows subtlety unavailable elsewhere. But **even rudimentary knowledge of a discipline** opens thoughts to criticism.

### Why think critically?

Criticism **sharpens thoughts "to a surgical degree."** Each field tests thoughts:

- **If they survive**, unscathed or through adaptation and qualification, we can rely on them with more confidence.
- **If they fail**, we come out **more awake to our former limits and open to new thoughts.**

## Chapter summary in one paragraph

Thinking happens in **complete thoughts**, made of a **subject**, which picks a particular thing out of a horizon of experience, and a **predicate**, which places that subject in a relevant, intelligible context. The **copula** ("being") joins them productively into a new entity that isn't reducible to its signs. Complete thoughts are the basic unit of language and thought, and they are **non-representational**: they raise experience into intelligibility instead of mirroring facts. **Sentences** are complete thoughts as *sensed*, and complete thoughts are sentences *understood*. The thinker isn't a self-sufficient ego who originates thoughts. We *belong to* thoughts that **radiate from subject matters**, and we are **responsible** for them anyway. Thinking becomes **critical** when it turns reflexive, using academic disciplines to set our own thoughts in context and expose their limits.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Complete thought** | A subject combined with a predicate. The basic unit of thought and language |
| **Thinking** | Combining and separating subjects and predicates |
| **Sentential subject** | Picks out a particular thing from a horizon of experience. Answers "What is being talked about?" |
| **Predicate** | What's said about a subject. Places it in an intelligible context (its "predicament"). Answers "What is being said about it?" |
| **Copula** | The link ("to be") that unites subject and predicate and produces a new being, the meaning |
| **Horizon of experience** | The background from which things stand out |
| **Objectify / subjectify** | Making a sliver of experience into a speakable topic |
| **Reification** | Treating an abstraction as a concrete thing. Fine unless forgotten |
| **Problem of reference** | How words supposedly bridge mind and reality. Dissolved by rejecting the representational model |
| **Sign vs. word** | Dictionary entries are signs. They become words only in use in sentences |
| **Semiotics vs. semantics** | Study of signs vs. study of meanings |
| **Field of intelligibility** | A context of meaning made up of forms of life, communities of practice, and traditions |
| **Concatenation** | Stringing complete thoughts into series (arguments, narratives). Relation, not synthesis |
| **Critical thinking** | Reflexive, metacognitive self-criticism through disciplinary lenses |
| **Standpoint** | The embodied, partial location of a perspective |

## Instructor's suggestions (Appendix, p. 214)

Alongside the ["three things"](../00-Word-Phrase-Sentence-Strategy/README.md) protocol:

1. **"Yoda grammar."** Show clips of Yoda. Students work out the usual English order of sentence parts and how Yoda rearranges them, using **subject, copula, predicate**. Yoda uses **predicate, subject, copula**, or in linguistic terms **object, subject, verb** (Lafrance 2015).
   - Revisit whether **"everything"** can be a subject, since by definition subjects pick out specific things from everything else. What other words fail as subjects?
   - Some words fail as **predicates** too, such as **"being," "space," and "time."** Have students try to form sentences with those as predicates.
2. **Think, pair, share** on a question the chapter leaves open: **Do thoughts originate in us (the thinkers), in the gods (as Spinoza and Aristotle suggest), or in subject matters themselves?** Why or why not?
3. **Reiterate** that thinking becomes "critical" by being **reflexive about the specific context** in which a thought takes place, using any of the disciplines or methods available.

## Discussion questions

- Try to make "nothing," "the universe," or "being itself" the subject of a sentence. What happens?
- Pick a title someone holds (coach, prophet, president). What field of intelligibility does the title need in order to make sense?
- Is "Your keys are not on the counter" a combination or a separation? Why does Dickman insist on *separation*?
- Name a discipline you know well. How could you use it to criticize one of your own recent thoughts?
