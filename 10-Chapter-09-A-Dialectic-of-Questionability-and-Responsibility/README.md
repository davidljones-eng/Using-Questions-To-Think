## The big idea

Chapter 9 completes the book's three-part pattern. Chapter 3 described a logic for thinking and Chapter 6 a rationality for reasoning. This chapter describes a **dialectic for dialogue**. It covers four topics:

1. The **four orientations** of questioning in dialogue: antithesis-, thesis-, synthesis-, and dialectics-centered.
2. **Erotetic logic** (questions as epistemic imperatives) gets its depth from dialogical dialectics. Every question, even one used only to get an answer, is rooted in genuine questioning.
3. **Thinking is internalized dialogue**, much as silent reading is internalized reading aloud.
4. **Responsibility**, meaning the ability to respond, comes from **being in question**. It doesn't come from a "call," and it isn't about being "answerable." **Dictatorial monologue** is the absence of genuine questions, and it amounts to evading responsibility.

> In asking a genuine question, "I do not necessarily intend to know something, but instead I intend to **hear what someone else has to say**… in such a way that something new and unpredictable emerges for the both of us."

### Dialectics is more than a celebration of difference

- Dickman's emphasis on dialectics is meant to resist a **naïve celebration of "infinite dialogical cacophonies"** (Bakhtin 1981).
- Reaching agreement includes respect for differences, but **dialogue is more than a practice of recognizing differences**. **Dialectical dialogues uncover truths within or across differences.** They are neither assimilative hegemony nor an exercise in noting diversity.
- **Etymology of "dialectics":**

| Part | Origin | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ***dia-*** | Greek | "across, through," and also "**thoroughly, entirely**" (Dickman's example: *diaper*, "thoroughly white") |
| ***-lekt-*** | from ***legein*** | to speak, to gather |
| ***-tics*** | from ***technē*** | art or craft (the root of *technology*) |

So **dialectics is a technical kind of dialogue**: in part, *a systematic method of reasoning with contradictions and conflicting interpretations, aimed at reaching truths through the exchange.*

## 1. How to question dialogues from at least four directions (pp. 191–194)

### Exposing pseudo-dialogue

- **Questions don't technically *say* anything**, so they're easy to misrecognize and forget, even though nothing said could be understood without them.
- Thoughts and arguments get cut off from their questions, and **dialogues get cut off from the questions that start, sustain, and complete them**.
- Dickman says he isn't inventing new kinds of dialogue. His claim is that **unless we value, and even venerate, questioning as the basic context of dialogue, we aren't experiencing our dialogues *as* dialogues.**

> **Question-less dialogue is pseudo-dialogue.**

### The full parallel

| Domain | Questioning coordinates… | …into | Questions open… |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thinking | subjects + predicates | complete thoughts | subjects → **predicative** possibilities |
| Reasoning | premises + conclusions | arguments | premises → **conclusive** possibilities |
| **Dialogue** | **theses + antitheses** | **fusions of horizons** | theses/antitheses → **synthetic** possibilities |

### The four orientations in dialogue

```
                              DIALOGUE
     [ THESIS ] ───────▶ [ ANTITHESIS ] ───────▶ [ SYNTHESIS ]
         ▲                     ▲                       ▲
         │                     │                       │
 (2) Thesis-centered   (1) Antithesis-centered  (3) Synthesis-centered
  "How did we get      "Why do we live in a     "Where are they going
   here again?"         world based on money     with this?"
                        anyway?"

        (4) Dialectics-centered: the dynamics of dialectics itself
            "What if we never get there?"
```

Each orientation has a **deflating** reading, which stops the conversation, and a **productive** reading, which moves it forward.

| # | Orientation | Looks toward | Example question | ✗ Deflating / conversation stopper | ✓ Productive |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Antithesis-centered** | the possibilities for **negation** or reaction that a thesis makes available | In a dialogue about a **70% progressive marginal income tax**: "**Why do we live in a world based on money anyway?**" | **Nihilating negativity**: deflationary cynicism, probably from someone with financial privilege, that undermines the topic itself | **Productive negativity**: asked *together*, it reveals possibilities for imagination and action that may be **even more progressive** than a tax rate, possibilities the dialogue had hidden |
| 2 | **Thesis-centered** | **back** to the thesis that set the dialogue off | "**How did we get here again?**" | — | A **reconstructive** retracing. We form a **hypo-thesis** (a little less than a pure thesis), and returning from the tangent exposes us to the thesis's **otherness**, because both it and we have changed |
| 3 | **Synthesis-centered** | **forward** to completion and fulfillment | "**Where are they going with this?**" "Is the dialogue complete? Have we covered everything?" | Impatience: a wish that the other person would **stop talking** | **Anticipatory excitement** (Anderson 2001). We can't help projecting fulfillment and then correcting it against what is actually said. That's why **we feel surprise**. Without anticipation we'd just pile up information |
| 4 | **Dialectics-centered** (dialogue-centered) | the **dynamics of dialectics itself** | "Is the triad the best model? Is a complete dialogue even possible? **What if we never get there?**" | Loss of courage, writing off everything as failure without total resolution. This is **Romanticism's "utopic completion"** delusion (West 2008): *If we can't have the whole thing, what's the point?* | Real engagement: "**Maybe the grand conversation will continue without us? So what?**" Gratitude for how far we've come, and attention to what we could do better next |

### Notes on each orientation

**Antithesis first?** This can seem backwards, but negativity often dominates our experience: war, deaths of loved ones, breakups. Losses **accumulate** over a life, and some people end up in **cynicism or nihilism**. We worry about pitfalls and fix on what we don't have yet. We forget that **negation presupposes a prior positivity**. **In this negativity we *wake up* to a dialogue that is already underway.** If we only want to stay where we are, as if nothing were moving, we arrive too late.

**Thesis: memory is constructed.** Retracing happens in the present. It isn't transparent access to the past. Eyewitness testimony is unreliable for the same reason. Our "memory" of the thesis is **produced, not recovered**.

> **"We can only really know a thesis from within the negativity of the antithesis."**

**Dialectics-centered** questioning is what the whole book has been doing (Jameson 2009). It's a different question from the anticipatory "**Are we there yet?**"

> **Dialectics-centered questioning resists the temptation of the "final answer."**
> "**The final answer makes questioning absurd**" (Cage 1973). "**The final question makes no answers absurd, but opens to ever further answering.**"

## 2. Erotetic logic has its depth in dialogical dialectics (pp. 194–197)

### Dialectics of question and answer, not just a logic

- Erotetic logic and dialectics **aren't opposites needing a compromise**. Neither are "question" and "answer."
- **Each has its own intrinsic dialectics**, and the two inform each other. **When genuine questioning meets genuine answering, the movement unfolds as dialectical dialogue.**
- **Plato's dialogues don't end in a complete, comprehensive answer.** The ***Lysis*** asks about true friendship and leaves readers **dwelling with the question**, inviting them to take up their **own responsibility** for questioning and answering.

### Thinking is internalized dialogue

- Thinking combines and separates subjects and predicates in light of the questions they answer. So **the thinker is split into the I as questioner and the I as answerer** (compare **Buber**). The two roles **take turns** inside us.
- **Thinking is an internalized dialogue.** We can't take in everything we know at a glance, so thinking presents itself to itself "as if in an inner dialogue with itself" (Gadamer).
- **Plato:**
  - ***Theaetetus*** (189e–190a): thinking is "a talk which the soul has with itself… **asks itself questions and answers them itself, affirms and denies**."
  - ***Sophist*** (263e): "Are not thought and speech the same, except that what we call thought is speech that occurs **without the voice**, inside the soul in conversation with itself?" "Of course."

### Objections and replies

| Objection | Reply |
|---|---|
| "I think **nonverbally, in pictures**" (Wittgenstein's *Tractatus*, compared with the *Investigations*) | **Imagination ≠ thought.** You can picture a triangle, but you can only *understand* a **chiliagon** (1,000 sides). Use your imagination, just don't **smear** the two together |
| "The **body** has its own intelligibility, prior to cognition" (Vilhauer 2016) | Thought may turn out to be **a function of embodiment**, but reducing it to embodiment commits the **genetic fallacy**. Dance and athletics are rigorous and take concentration, yet **thinking is something different**. **Thought is a new phenomenon that emerges from the adapting awareness that infuses the human *habitus***, through internalization and reflection |

### The reading analogy

| Stage | Reading | Thinking |
|---|---|---|
| **Social origin** | We first read **aloud**, *for someone*: guardians read to us, and we read to a teacher who assesses us (Raphael 1986) | We first question and answer **with others** in dialogue |
| **Transitivity** | "To read" takes a direct object (*something*) and also an **indirect one: someone to whom it is read** | Thinking is addressed to someone |
| **Performance** | Reading aloud is like **musical performance**. It brings words into harmony with meaning. Think of a guardian giving the **wolf and the three little pigs** different voices | — |
| **Diagnosis** | A **monotone** reading voice signals sentences *perceived* but not complete thoughts *understood* | — |
| **Internalization** | **Silent reading is reading *to ourselves***, split into the I who reads and the I who is read to. We're recognized as **literate** when we can do this alone | **Thinking is the internalized practice and play of dialogue** |

### Therapeutic implications

- How we speak with others shapes how we speak with **ourselves**, and that matters for **mental well-being**.
- **Clinical therapy** depends on **internalizing the therapist's voice**. Changing our *beliefs* (as in CBT) isn't enough. **We have to change our inner dialogues.**
- **Does your conscience condemn you with demands and accusations, or could it ask you *genuine questions*?** (Dickman 2018b)

### Plato's dialogues as therapeutic scripts

- Plato is known, and often criticized, for arguments about the **immortality of the soul** and the **Forms**. Critics start with Aristotle, and "**Platonist**" has been used as a reproach.
- But **why did Plato write dialogues** instead of plain arguments? The genre must be **essential** to what he teaches (Moors 1978).
- The dialogues belong to the ancient genre of **aretology**, a kind of biography in which authors use artistic license to present the hero at their best, writing what the hero *would or should* have said in order to capture their ***aretē*** (virtue). Compare the **Christian gospels** and **Diogenes Laertius**'s *Lives of the Philosophers*.
- Plato gives us a hero to **emulate, imitate, and internalize** through habituation. Reading the dialogues **initiates us into dialogical excellence, which is excellence in thinking.**

> **Socrates is our regulative ideal for what it is to be a thinker.**

## 3. Responsibility emerges through dialogue (pp. 197–201)

### Against "answerable animals"

- In an age of answers, the point of questioning **isn't more or better answers**.
- Dickman objects to describing us as "**answerable animals**" or to saying we must "**give an account of oneself**" (Butler 2005).

> **Questioning is the fertile ground in which responsibility flourishes.** Only through questioning are we **able to respond, *response-able***. Our effort to be and to become is supported by dialectical dialogue rooted in genuine questioning.

- This goes against the inherited view of what makes responsibility possible. **We aren't called into action by a "call"** (as in "the call of conscience"). **We are "*in question*."**

### Three theories of "the call," and Dickman's critique

| Thinker | The "call" | How it constitutes the subject | Problem for Dickman |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Althusser** (1994) / **Butler** (1997) | **Interpellation**: being hailed ("Hey, you!") | Only through the call does one become a "you." **Subjection** constitutes the subject | Responsibility is reduced to being called and subjected |
| **Levinas** | **The face of the other** calls me to responsibility and demands that I live up to "the adventure of subjectivity" | The face shows suffering that requires a response and makes my activities public. "Responsibility extending beyond intention's 'range of action'… characterizes the subjective existence capable of discourse." Humanity **dawns** on me and calls my egocentrism into question (Blum 1983). Solitary self-examination can't do this. The call appeals to me as **response-ability**, not as a source of information | **Asymmetry**: the other calls "**from a height**," something close to **divine proclamation**. The structure is one of **accusation**, and I respond as **guilty**. There's **disjunction, not mutuality or reciprocity** |
| **Heidegger** | **Conscience**: a call from the **otherness within oneself** (Ricoeur) | "'It' calls, against our expectations… The call comes **from me, and yet over me**." It **demands** authenticity and pulls *Dasein* out of "**the they**" and their "**idle chatter**." The caller is **uncanny**, answers to no name, and its authority **can't be questioned**. "It goes against this kind of being… to be drawn into any consideration and talk." **Counter-discourse is impossible.** We're "guilty" (not morally) of being **thrown into being with no recourse to "the" answer**. The call frees us to take up our own possibilities, and **we become our own ground (the ground of a nullity)** | An **unquestionable** authority that **forbids dialogue** |

### Anxiety opens us to language

- **Anxiety** points to a feeling different from guilty subjection. **Gadamer:** the human task is "to maintain a **unity of self, integrity**, within ever threatening disintegration into boundless chaos."
- Our effort to be keeps **projecting possibilities** even in horror. Anxiety can lead to **care and reflection** instead of paralysis.
- **The gift of anxiety:** it **distances us from things and opens us to language, especially dialogue.**
  - **Gadamer:** "It is through the *logos*… that a person is able to think something and at the same time **hold certain possibilities open**."
  - **Charles Taylor:** "**The human agent exists in a space of questions.**"
  - **Milan Kundera** (Tereza, *The Unbearable Lightness of Being*): "**Questions set the limits of distinctively human possibilities.**"
  - **Irigaray:** "Humans give themselves divine representations [or answers] as supports for becoming… But what are these worth if they do not favor natural growth?"

### Orientation toward the Good

- Anxiety shows our perspectives to be **incomplete**, and that incompleteness gives potentially infinite chances to **reorient toward the good** instead of making egocentric grabs for dominance.
- **Taylor:** "**To know who I am is a species of knowing where I stand.** My identity is defined by the commitments and identifications which provide the frame or horizon within which I can try to determine… what is good."
- In anxiety we see that **our time is limited**, so we have to **prioritize** and decide as well as we can with the information we have. Deciding with an eye to the Good means **we can't avoid dialogue with ourselves and others**.
- **Dialogue requires self-forgetting**, a release from defensive constructions of the self so we can follow the *logos* toward the good. **Gadamer:** "**One must lose oneself in order to find oneself.**"

### Levinas: an ethics of sacrifice

- Desperate self-preservation, the anxious need to "take care" of oneself, blocks what Levinas calls an **ethics of sacrifice**: "Sacrifice cannot find a place for itself in an order divided between the authentic and the inauthentic."
- Taking responsibility for others, **even to the point of dying for them**, signals a **real transcendence within life**. It shows something **beyond being**: there's more to life than self-preservation, though not an "afterlife."
- **Only through responsibility for others does a truly human "I" emerge**, one that is **neither a substantialist identity nor resolute authenticity**.

### No complete information, no perfect authority

- There's **no complete body of information** for decisions about the Good, and **no one we can defer to with complete confidence**. **Gadamer:** "One has to ask oneself, and in so doing, one necessarily finds oneself in discussion with oneself or with another."
- **Taylor on the transcendental condition of meaning:** "Do I know what I'm saying?… I can only meet [this challenge] by **confronting my thought and language with the thought and reactions of others**… the very confidence that we know what we mean… **depends on this relating**… We are inducted into language by being brought to see things as our tutors do. Later… we can deviate."
- **Relating to others constitutes me as both *responsive* and *responsible*.**

### Humility about understanding

- The framework of responsibility **humbles our hubris**. We're tempted to **smear understanding over everything** and to expect everything to be understandable.
- **We can only understand discourse, in dialogue with others, through the opening of genuine questions.** The best we can do is **dig down to our fundamental questions**, where we begin turning experience into discourse.
- Some things aren't understandable, not because of my personal limits but because **they simply aren't the kind of thing that can be understood**.

> **Dickman's version of the Serenity Prayer:** "May we have the serenity to accept what we cannot understand, the courage to understand what we can, and the wisdom to know the difference between these two."

## 4. Dictatorial monologue lacks genuine questions (pp. 201–203)

### Parallel monologues

- It's hard to imagine a conversation with **no questions**. **Two parallel monologues don't make a dialogue.** Living *alongside* someone isn't living *with* them.
- **Aristotle:** "In this case of human beings what seems to count as living together is this **sharing in dialogue and thought**, not sharing the same pasture, as in the case of **grazing animals**" (*NE* 1170b10–15).

> **"Our age of answers is really an age of monologues."**

- We haven't recovered from the **monological age of religious dictates** or the **monological age of Eurocentric "Reason."**
- **Neoliberal and advanced-capitalist interests** flood us with answers:
  - **Advertisements** tell us what we need.
  - **Political leaders** tell us what we need.
  - **Academic researchers** produce piles of **publications nobody reads**, to earn tenure.
- We're given **answers to questions nobody is really asking** (DRIP again).

### Oppression

- **Dictatorial monologue oppresses dialogical responsibility and the freedom it produces.** Oppression **cuts off our freedom to enhance and amplify our existence** (Beauvoir 2015).
- **Nature doesn't oppress.** It provides the *friction* of experience that gives us things to think about. **Only other people can oppress**, because they **force us to take sides**.
  - "**Either you are with us, or you are against us.**" Hostile or ally?
  - Division like this **turns others into things** (Yancy 2015). It's often disguised as "**only natural**": women as "the weaker sex," racial stereotypes.
- **The core attitude of dictatorial monologue (Beauvoir):** "**You are oppressing me by not allowing me to oppress you!**"
  - *Example:* some US Southerners call the Civil War "**the War of Northern Aggression**," as though trying to free enslaved people were an act of aggression.
- **Its assumption: we're only really free if we deny someone else's freedom.**

### Why we're susceptible

**1. Complicity in our own oppression (Beauvoir), as in Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor**

- The Grand Inquisitor (*The Brothers Karamazov*) holds that humans **can't be trusted with freedom** and **don't really want it** when they can have **food and amusement**.
- People prefer **feeling happy** to freedom and responsibility. Why do the work of being responsible for yourself when you can turn to the church for answers?
- From his perspective, humans **don't eat to live, they live to eat**. They seek **gratification instead of the struggle to flourish**. (Compare the Introduction's *eudaimonia* as activity, not feeling.)

**2. Our "tragic ambiguity" (Beauvoir)**

- We are **radical subjects of experience**, individual I's that systematically escape categorization. **At the same time we are objects for others.**
- That's why **gossip is easy**: we make people the *topic* of conversation instead of engaging them in dialogue.
- **We do it to ourselves** too, when we treat ourselves as objects with an **essence**. That brings a certain **comfort**, a relief from the struggle of responsibility.

> **"The final answer, the final solution, is intrinsically a resistance to and an evasion of the responsibility"** (Beauvoir).
> **Dictatorial monologue, which treats provisional answers as absolute, is an evasion of genuine questions and dialogue.**

Dickman notes there's more to say here and points to further research. He then turns to the Conclusion, **a meditation on the joy of genuine questions**.

## Chapter summary in one paragraph

Dialectics is the **technical craft of dialogue**, a way to uncover truths *across* differences, not just to celebrate them. Questioning in dialogue radiates in **four directions**. **Antithesis-centered** questions can be nihilating ("Why money at all?" said as cynicism) or productive, opening wider possibilities. **Thesis-centered** questions ("How did we get here?") reconstruct a hypothesis that we only know from inside the antithesis. **Synthesis-centered** questions ("Where is this going?") express anticipation, which is what makes surprise possible. **Dialectics-centered** questions ("What if we never get there?") resist the romantic demand for a final answer, because "the final answer makes questioning absurd." Erotetic logic gets its **depth** from these dialectics. Plato's *Lysis* ends in a question handed to the reader. **Thinking is internalized dialogue** between the I as questioner and the I as answerer, just as silent reading is internalized reading aloud. That's why therapy works by changing our *inner dialogues*, and why Plato's dialogues, read as **aretology**, train us to internalize **Socrates as the regulative ideal of a thinker**. **Responsibility** doesn't come from a "call," whether Althusser's and Butler's interpellation, Levinas's accusing face, or Heidegger's unquestionable conscience. It comes from **being in question**. Anxiety opens us to language and to a "space of questions" (Taylor), oriented toward the Good, which we can only pursue **in dialogue** and through **self-forgetting**. Relating to others makes us **responsive and responsible** and humbles our urge to understand everything. **Dictatorial monologue**, the real face of our "age of answers," oppresses by forcing sides and turning people into things ("You oppress me by not letting me oppress you"). It thrives on our complicity (the Grand Inquisitor) and our tragic ambiguity, and as the **final answer** it is an **evasion of responsibility**.

## Instructor's suggestions (Appendix, p. 219)

Alongside the ["three things"](../00-Word-Phrase-Sentence-Strategy/README.md) protocol:

1. **Practice the four orientations**: **antithesis-centered, thesis-centered, synthesis-centered, and dialogue-centered**. Bring in a few dialogue excerpts, **perhaps from Plato**, to get students thinking about the parts of dialogues.
2. **Think, pair, share:** *How are we made responsible, as in able to respond? Are we **called** to authentic responsibility, or **asked a question** to which we respond?* (See the sections on Heidegger and Levinas.) *Is there an underlying "I" who can choose to take responsibility, or is the "I" a function of already being responsible?*
3. **Think, pair, share:** *How does oppression interfere with freedom? Are there ways of speaking with others that are oppressive? How so?*

### Practice template: four orientations for a dialogue

Dialogue so far: *"Our town should build a new stadium." / "But the schools need funding more."*

| Orientation | Productive question | Deflating version to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Antithesis-centered | Why are we treating these as the only two options? What else could public money do? | "Why bother with public projects at all?" (cynical stop) |
| Thesis-centered | How did the stadium proposal come up in the first place? What need was it answering? | — |
| Synthesis-centered | Where might this be heading, and what would a good resolution feel like to both of us? | "Where are you going with this?" (= stop talking) |
| Dialectics-centered | Is "stadium vs. schools" even the right framing? What if we never fully resolve it? | "If we can't agree, what's the point?" |

## Discussion questions

- Recall a time you said "How did we get here?" in a conversation. Did retracing change the original topic for you?
- What does your inner voice sound like: accusing, demanding, or questioning? How would you rewrite one of its accusations as a genuine question?
- Do you experience responsibility more as being *called* or as being *in question*? Does the difference matter?
- Where in your life are you complicit with "dictatorial monologue," taking answers so you don't have to bear the questions?
