## Part II opener: "Make Questions Explicit for Reasoning" (pp. 67–69)

**Part II's two topics:** **reasoning** and **questioning**. The first step is to relate *thought* to *reason*. Are they the same thing under two names? Can you think without reasoning, or reason without thinking?

### From authority to reason

- Historically there was a shift, often credited to the Greeks but also found in ancient South Asia, East Asia, and Central America, **from appeals to supernatural authority to appeals to reason**.
- The earliest appeals took the form of **religious or mythic dictates**, which Lincoln (1996) calls *authoritarian discourse*. Examples are Greek **oracles** and the Chinese **mandate of Tian** (heaven).
- It **persists today**. In mid-2018, defending the separation of refugee families at the US–Mexico border, Attorney General **Jeff Sessions cited Romans 13** (obey the government because God ordained it).
- When reason rises, "*Why* do we believe/do what we do?" gets answered by **logical inferences, principled grounds, and scientific and natural evidence**.

### Etymology

- **Reason** comes from the Latin ***reri***, "to reckon, to think." Its Greek counterpart is ***logos***, which ranges over discourse, language, reason, and logic. It's also the suffix **"-logy"** ("the study of") in socio*logy*, bio*logy*, and so on.

1. The elements of reasoning, **premises and conclusions** (Ch. 4)

## The big idea

Dickman stipulates a definition modeled on Chapter 1's account of thinking:

> **Reasoning is the activity of combining or separating multiple complete thoughts in a specific way called *inference*.**
> An **argument** is the "atom" of reasoning. Its two "particles" are the **premise** and the **conclusion**.

| Level | Particles | Bond | Unit | Perceived as / understood as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Thinking** | subject + predicate | **copula** | complete thought | **sentence** (perceived) / **complete thought** (understood) |
| **Reasoning** | premise(s) + conclusion | **inference** (relevant relation) | **argument** | **exposition** (perceived) / **argument** (understood) |

An argument isn't just "a pile of sentences." A **specific relation** between the sentences makes it an argument.

## 1. One cannot "win" an argument (pp. 72–77)

### "Argument is war": a dead metaphor

- In everyday speech, "fighting" and "arguing" are used interchangeably.
- **Lakoff & Johnson:** this isn't just a way of talking. It's how we **model and experience** arguing. We "**defend**" positions, "**attack**" arguments, "**bring out the big guns**." If fights can be won or lost, so can arguments.
- It's a **dead metaphor**, so pervasive we barely notice it's a metaphor, and it's hard to take alternatives seriously. It's **partial**: it highlights some aspects and **hides** others. Dickman's playful alternative: what if argument were modeled on "**doggy-paddling in a swimming pool**"?
- **Whose interests does it serve?** Dickman's answer: **patriarchal interests and restrictive gender roles**.

### Adversarialism and patriarchy (Burrow 2010)

- The adversarial model sits in a culture where **masculinity = aggressiveness** and **femininity = relationality** (think of the slur "you throw like a girl").
- Aggressive vocabulary: "sparring," "shooting down the opponent."
- **Win-lose tactics:**
  - testing claims against the **most extreme opposition** instead of building better arguments together
  - hunting for **contradictions** and **exaggerated thought experiments**, then cynically rejecting a whole position if it can't handle every extreme case
  - **not listening**, just scanning for contradictions ("**trigger-happy** with contradiction accusations": partisans calling each other hypocrites)
- This counts as "good" argument both in **social-media frenzies** and in **professional academia**.
- **Harms:**
  - Women and gender-nonconforming people are **alienated** from disciplines like philosophy by adversarial classrooms and conferences.
  - A **double standard**: women who argue adversarially are judged "**too aggressive**." People are bullied to conform and punished if they don't.
  - Bias against women and minority professors in **course evaluations and grade challenges** (Mitchell & Martin 2018).
  - People who use **politeness tactics are subordinated**. People out to win use any tactic.
- **If winning is what matters, what happens to *truth*?**

(Part III develops **collaborative** models such as dialogue.)

### Dickman's stipulation

> **An argument is not adversarial opposition but the fundamental unit of reasoning**: at least **two complete thoughts** (a premise and a conclusion) in a **relevant relation**, better known as **logical inference**.

### Exposition vs. argument

- **Exposition:** a group of nearby sentences that *may seem* relevantly related.
- **An argument is an exposition understood. An exposition is an argument perceived.**
- **Cues:** conclusion indicators ("therefore," "thus") and premise indicators ("because," "since"). You can see the cues and still miss the argument, either because you don't grasp the complete thoughts or because you don't follow the inferences.
- **Just as questioning is needed to understand a sentence as a complete thought, questioning is needed to understand an exposition as an argument.** Without understanding, it's only an *alleged* argument.

**Why this undermines "winning":** an argument is first of all **a list of sentences**. **It doesn't matter whose sentences they are**, "yours" or "mine." So winners and losers are hard to identify.

### Reasons ≠ motives

- "Reason" is ambiguous, like "bank." Here it means **argument**, not **psychological motivation**.
- A **motive** has a linear causal relation to an action: they did this *because of* that.
- Appeals to motive are often used to **explain away** what someone says. For example, women are dismissed unfairly by being accused of having **PMS**.
- **An explanation is not an argument.**

### What reasoning requires

**Reasoning** = an attempt to give **adequate justification or warrant** for a belief or action, including working out inferential relations and the principles behind them. It needs:

| Capacity | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Articulation** | Elaborating and **defining terms**. Spelling each idea out as a complete thought (subject + predicate) **in line with specific questions**. Avoiding buzzwords, vague words, and jargon. Stipulating definitions and making the implicit explicit. *Caveat:* definitions are often **results** of dialogue, not preconditions of it. |
| **Reflection** | **Stepping back** from the competing forces in a situation, so we aren't simply *caused* to act by nonrational influences |

**On reflection:**

- **Thoughts are as "real" as falling rocks.** They're part of the universe's causal network, but we call the relations between thoughts **"reasons" or "inferences."**
- **Premises don't "cause" conclusions like falling dominoes or pool balls.** Reflection **clears a space** where we can draw out inferential relations.
- Reflection is **consciousness of consciousness**, our awareness of the **questionability** of phenomena (Ch. 3).
- It isn't a "free will" outside causality. It's a **natural, dialectical process**, a **seesaw** in which experience informs consciousness and consciousness informs experience.
- **Hegel:** consciousness looks for certainty in **immediate sensation** but is "**pulled up short**." Sense-certainty yields only "**this, here, now**," which is too empty to count as knowledge. So we realize we need **mediation: concepts and predicates**.
- **Baudrillard:** a 1:1 map showing every detail would be useless. **Models help us reach what matters.**
- **Reflection** = consciousness overcoming frustration by **creating and applying models and concepts**.

### What is NOT an argument

| Not an argument | Why / note |
|---|---|
| **Explanations / accounts** | Show *why* something is so, not *that* it is (or probably is). Clues: common knowledge, past events. Arguments can support choosing one explanation over another |
| **Aphorisms** ("deep thoughts") | Arguments can be *interpreted out of* them through rumination |
| **Definitions** | Not even complete thoughts |
| **Summaries and lists** | Convey information, not in premise→conclusion order |
| **Reports about an argument** | **Mention ≠ use.** Mentioning something isn't endorsing it |
| **Illustrations / examples** | Can clarify a claim or serve as a premise, but don't support anything on their own |
| **Unsupported assertions / unwarranted assumptions** | e.g., "People are not afraid of dying; they are afraid of never having lived." Assumptions come from experience or tradition; **stereotypes** are assumptions gone wrong |
| **Conditional statements** ("If it rains, the picnic is canceled") | Antecedent ≠ premise, consequent ≠ conclusion, though sometimes shorthand for an inference |

None of these is an argument because their sentences **don't stand in the supportive relevant relation called inference**.

## 2. Conclusions are supported complete thoughts (pp. 77–79)

- A conclusion is a **complete thought** (subject + predicate).
  - Its **subject** is **what the whole argument is about**.
  - Its **predicate** is **what the whole argument sets out to support**.
- **Conclusions** are what arguers want us to **believe, agree to, or do**. **Premises** are the **means**.
- **One argument has only one conclusion**, though that conclusion can serve as a premise in other arguments.
- **Indicators:** therefore, thus, hence, it follows that, consequently, this shows that…
- **Position doesn't matter.** Conclusions needn't come last. An essay's **thesis** often opens the essay. We expect conclusions at the end because of other uses of the word (stories, songs, ceremonies), but **a conclusion is defined by its relevance to its premises, not by where it sits**.

### Two aspects of a conclusion

**(1) Closure: *con-* + *-clude***

- ***con-*** = together, with (concept, concern, concatenation, **concierge** = fellow servant, **concentric** = sharing a center, concert)
- ***-clude*** = Latin for closing, fastening, shutting, terminating
- A conclusion **brings a subject–predicate relation together in a closure**. It is a **closure of our horizon of possible commitments**, limiting what we can commit to or believe.
- Insofar as it's supported, it's **true (or probably true)**, and insofar as it's true, it **delimits** possibilities for further knowledge.
- **Does this limit freedom?** No. Determinations **open** possibilities. Without them we'd have "**chaotic scrambling**," not freedom to think differently. **Arguments set up boundaries within which we are free to play further.**

**(2) Yield**

- The **agricultural** sense: we till, weed, wait, and harvest what the field **yields**. **Conclusions are yielded by premises, like fruit from trees.**
- This also accounts for "yield" as **surrender**: when we reach a conclusion we agree with, we **give way and stop contesting the premises**.
- **Conclusions indicate flourishing arguments.**
- A provocative aside: what would a **labor analysis of argumentation** look like? *Who owns the means of producing conclusions?*

> **A supported conclusion is both a delimitation and a yield.** It grows out of premises, and once it bears fruit it delimits our lifeworld's horizon.

## 3. Premises are supportive complete thoughts (pp. 79–82)

- Premises are complete thoughts that **support** (prop up, bring forward, sustain) the conclusion. They make it **stand out** from the general field of intelligibility.
- **They depend on each other:** without premises, a conclusion is just an **independent thought**. Without a conclusion, premises aren't premises.
- Premises "**lead to**" conclusions, and conclusions "**follow from**" premises. Does that mean linear, domino causality?

### Etymology: *pre-* + *-mise*

- ***pre-*** = before. ***-mise*** = Latin for **sending on a mission**. Premises **set reasons before us**.
- History of the word:
  1. **late-medieval logic and grammar**: prior propositions from which another follows
  2. **legal deeds and wills**: matters stated earlier in the document
  3. since those matters were mostly **land and households**, early-modern Europe came to use "premises" for **houses, buildings, grounds** (as in criminals "entering the premises")

### Premises as *grounds*

- We **build on** premises, and structures **stand on** them. Shaky land or shallow foundations mean the house blows over. **Conclusions have strong or shaky foundations depending on their premises.**
- **The surprising direction:** we didn't extend "ground" from land to logic. **Logic's premise–conclusion relation helped us see a non-linear relation between land and dwelling.**
- That relation **escapes Aristotle's four causes**:

| Aristotle's cause | House example |
|---|---|
| Efficient | the builder |
| Formal | the blueprint |
| Material | the wood, etc. |
| Final | sheltering the people who dwell there |

The **ground** "causes" the house in none of these ways. **The relation is interdependency and support, not linear or chronological causality.**

### Why the reason/motive distinction matters so much

- *In whose interest* is it to make us see reasons as mere motives? **It's easier to dismiss an argument as egocentric preference.**
- **Motives** are chronologically prior thoughts and feelings that **predictably cause** action ("Given that this person got angry, it's no surprise they shouted and broke a window"). Personality types and diagnoses make behavior seem predictable.
- **Law:** "**crimes of passion**" get lighter sentences than **calculated murder**, as if the calculator stood *above* the causal network and chose freely.
- We live in a **tension between commitment to free choice and commitment to causal orders**.
- **Kant's compromise**, which fits Dickman's view: **reasons stand in a completely different order from physical and psychological causality.** Whatever freedom we have **rests on our ability to reason autonomously**, even though we're bound up in causal networks.

> **Reasoning (arguments whose conclusions follow fittingly from reliable premises) is what sets us free.**
> Freedom isn't random spontaneity. It has **a pattern and order governed by rules** (Ch. 5).

### More about premises

- **Implicit premises** must be made **explicit** so the whole argument can be assessed (compare implied subjects in complete thoughts).
- **Premises are (or can be) conclusions of other arguments**, which have their own premises, and so on.
- This regress shows **a second orientation of reason** besides inference: **the quest for reliable fundamental premises, solid ground(s)**, which Kant calls reason's quest for **"the unconditioned."**
- **Axioms** are foundational premises assumed **within a system or field**. They **define the parameters of coherence and rationality** in that system (Ch. 5).
- **Kant's further point:** there must also be **principles that make *any* system possible**, shared across every relative system. Without them there could be no particular systems and no internal coherence (MacIntyre 1988).

## 4. Arguments are neither true nor false (pp. 82–88)

> **Stipulation: truth is a feature of complete thoughts, and *only* of complete thoughts.**

"True" has many uses, like "bank." Wittgenstein's **language games** explain why that's fine. Dickman identifies **three predominant uses** and a **shared phenomenological core** (Scharlemann 1981).

### Three kinds of truth

| Kind | Paradigm | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Correspondence truth** | sentence ↔ fact | A sentence, like a **picture**, **matches** a state of affairs (true) or doesn't (false). Assumes objective facts that words describe accurately or not (early Wittgenstein) | Both sides of **creationism vs. science** debates assume it |
| **Constancy truth** | integrity over time | **Reliability and consistency** across changing circumstances. Doesn't require facts, since the future hasn't happened | a "**true friend**"; steady, "true" **convictions**; an archer's "**true aim**" |
| **Alethic truth** (Greek *alētheia*) | un-hiddenness | *a-* (not) + *lēthē* (hiddenness, oblivion; the river **Lethe** makes souls forget past lives). **Not hiding, not forgetting.** "**Aha!**" moments. Something **stands out** from an undifferentiated background so we can't overlook it anymore | stories and art that "**ring true**" (Gadamer); fitting predicates that **reveal** a subject; **dis-covery** (un-covering) |

### The shared core: unity of identity and difference

| Truth | Difference | Identity |
|---|---|---|
| Correspondence | fact ≠ sentence | they **match** |
| Constancy | one time ≠ another time | the **integrity** across times |
| Alethic | the thing **stands out** (differentiation) | the **unified background / horizon** |

> **Our experience of truth is our experience of the unity of identity and difference.** Alethic truth runs the dynamic in **reverse**, but still keeps that unity.

### Dickman's preference

- **Ideally, integrate all three**: true complete thoughts that **correspond**, are **reliable**, and are **outstanding**.
- That may be too high a bar for finite thinkers, so **seek alethic truth first**: complete thoughts in which **predicates fittingly reveal subjects**.
- Alethic truth is **intrinsic to complete thoughts** (Descartes; Spinoza). There's **no need to map onto an "external" world**, which keeps the phenomenological bracket in place.
- **Dictionary definitions are neither true nor false.** They report current usage and aren't complete thoughts. A definition may be **correct** by current usage, and current usage may be "incorrect" next to past usage (look at how "**queer**" and "**awful**" have changed).

### Alethic truth as an antidote to the hegemony of correspondence

- *Whose interest* does it serve to treat correspondence as "true truth"?
- As science stresses correspondence, **poetry and the arts are marginalized**. Art programs are among the first cut from US school budgets.
- **Religion vs. science:** as long as both sides assume correspondence is the only truth, the debates will go on. **What if religious language fits *alethic* truth and scientific language fits *correspondence* truth?** That could **dissolve** the supposed debate.
- Correspondence truth has historically served the **adversarial model** tied to **patriarchy and racism**. Description is sometimes useful, but "we do not need to smear it across all times."

### Truth-preservation

- Truth belongs to complete thoughts, so **premises and conclusions must be complete thoughts**. We want to reason **from true premises to true conclusions**.
- **Arguments as such are neither true nor false.** The question is what **preserves** sentential truth. Some forms of reasoning are **truth-preserving**. Some expositions only *look* like reasoning and aren't.
- **Truth-preservation is the flip side of support:**
  - Premises **support** a conclusion when **their truth is preserved in it**.
  - A conclusion is **supported** when it **preserves the premises' truth**.

### Venn diagrams and the square of opposition

The four categorical statement types:

```
   A: All philosophers are mortal.  ←── contraries ──→  E: No philosophers are mortal.
                 ╲                                         ╱
                    ╲         contradictories           ╱
                    ╱                                   ╲
   I: Some philosophers are mortal.  ←─ subcontraries ─→  O: Some philosophers are not mortal.
```

(Dickman's text: the contrary of the "all" statement is "No philosophers are mortal." Its contradiction is "Some philosophers are not mortal.")

**Categorical syllogism:**

```
Premise 1:  All humans are mortal.
Premise 2:  All philosophers are human.
Conclusion: Therefore, all philosophers are mortal.
```

**Figure 1 (text version).** Three overlapping circles, two on the bottom and one on top (Humans, Philosophers, Mortals). Plot the premises by shading:

```
            ┌───────── Mortals ─────────┐
            │   ┌──── Humans ────┐       │
            │   │  ┌Philosophers┐│      │
            │   │  │            ││      │
            │   │  └────────────┘│      │
            │   └────────────────┘      │
            └───────────────────────────┘
   P1 shades out Humans-outside-Mortals; P2 shades out Philosophers-outside-Humans.
   Result: Philosophers lie wholly inside Mortals, so the conclusion is already "in" the premises.
```

- A conclusion is relevantly acceptable when **its plot is visibly identical to the plot of the premises**. The conclusion **preserves the content and truth** of the premises.
- **A proof** is essentially *a list of complete thoughts, relevantly related, that preserves truth throughout*.

### How to criticize an argument (preview)

1. **Are the premises even relevant to the conclusion?** If not, it's **not an argument, just an exposition**. Something can look like an argument and not be one, just as something can look like a complete thought and not be one.
2. **Do the premises rest on shaky foundations?** If the arguments behind the premises aren't reliably truth-preserving, be wary of this one too.
3. **Are the premises false?** Then there's **nothing to preserve**. The "premises" aren't premises, and the conclusion floats in "ontological or semantic outer space."

> **A refutation doesn't so much prove a conclusion wrong as show that what *looked like* an argument isn't one.**
> **Refutation dissolves an argument. It makes it disintegrate into a mere exposition.**

The open question, *what exactly is the relevant relation that turns an exposition into an argument?*, is answered in **Chapter 5**.

## Chapter summary in one paragraph

Reasoning combines and separates complete thoughts through **inference**, and its unit is the **argument**: at least one **premise** supporting one **conclusion**. The common "**argument is war**" metaphor is a dead, partial metaphor that serves **patriarchal, adversarial** interests, rewards contradiction-hunting over listening, and pushes truth below winning. Since an argument is really just a list of sentences regardless of whose they are, **no one can "win" one**. An **exposition** is an argument perceived, and an **argument** is an exposition understood, which takes questioning. **Reasons are not motives**: motives explain causally, while reasons belong to a different order (Kant), and that order is the basis of our **freedom**. Reasoning requires **articulation** and **reflection**. **Conclusions** are closures of our horizon of commitments and **yields** of their premises. **Premises** are **grounds** that support without linear causation, and the regress of premises points to reason's quest for **the unconditioned** and to **axioms**. **Truth** belongs only to complete thoughts and comes in three kinds (**correspondence, constancy, alethic**), all sharing a unity of identity and difference. Dickman prioritizes **alethic** truth. **Arguments aren't true or false but truth-preserving or not**, and refuting one dissolves it back into an exposition.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Reasoning** | Combining and separating complete thoughts through inference |
| **Argument** | At least two complete thoughts (premise + conclusion) in a relevant relation. The atom of reasoning |
| **Exposition** | Sentences that seem related. An argument *perceived* |
| **Inference** | The relevant, supportive relation between premises and conclusion |
| **Conclusion** | The supported complete thought. Closure plus yield |
| **Premise** | A supporting complete thought. A ground |
| **Reason vs. motive** | Justification in the order of inference vs. psychological cause |
| **Explanation** | Shows *why* something is so. Not an argument |
| **Articulation / reflection** | Defining terms explicitly / stepping back to clear a space for inference |
| **Mention vs. use** | Reporting a claim vs. endorsing it |
| **The unconditioned** | Kant: reason's quest for ultimate, unconditional grounds |
| **Axiom** | A foundational premise assumed within a system |
| **Correspondence truth** | Sentence matches fact |
| **Constancy truth** | Reliability over time |
| **Alethic truth** | Un-concealment. Something stands out ("rings true") |
| **Truth-preserving** | Premises' truth carried into the conclusion |
| **Proof** | Relevantly related complete thoughts that preserve truth throughout |
| **Refutation** | Dissolving an apparent argument into a mere exposition |
