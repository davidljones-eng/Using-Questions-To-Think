## Why the appendix exists

Dickman left chapter exercises and self-quizzes out of the book on purpose. Students could quiz themselves alone and never talk to anyone, and the book's whole argument is that thinking, reasoning, and understanding happen through **dialogue**. He doesn't want students memorizing and repeating facts, or drilling a fixed set of skill steps. He wants them practicing dialogue. So the appendix gives instructors one general strategy for discussion, plus suggestions for each chapter.

### Three ways to read the book

The appendix gives two reading orders:

| Order | Sequence | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Linear** | Intro → Ch. 1 → … → Ch. 9 → Conclusion | Follows the table of contents |
| **By layer** | Ch. **1, 4, 7** → Ch. **2, 5, 8** → Ch. **3, 6, 9** | (1) the *scaffold*: thought, reason, dialogue → (2) what *questioning* does in each domain → (3) the *underlying coordinates and principles* of each domain |

The Introduction adds a third option: read backward from Chapter 9 to Chapter 1. Later chapters "shed new light" on earlier ones, so any order works.

```
                 Scaffold (what it is)     Questions (what they do)     Principles (its logic)
Thinking   →     Ch. 1 Complete thoughts   Ch. 2 Questions & thoughts   Ch. 3 Logic of Q & A
Reasoning  →     Ch. 4 Explicit arguments  Ch. 5 Questions & arguments  Ch. 6 Rationality of Q & R
Dialogue   →     Ch. 7 Reconciliations     Ch. 8 Questions & dialogues  Ch. 9 Dialectic of Q & R
```

## The strategy itself ("three things")

Dickman adapted the strategy from the National School Reform Faculty's **"text rendering experience"** protocol (NSRF 2014). The sharing order comes from Ritchhart, Church & Morrison (2011, 207).

### Preparation (before class)

For each assigned reading, every student picks:

1. **A single word**
2. **A single phrase**
3. **A single sentence**

Each item must:

- have been especially *meaningful* to the student, ideally in relation to the day's topic as titled in the syllabus;
- carry an **author and page-number citation**;
- come with a **1–2 sentence explanation** of why it stood out.

Students came to call these the **"three things."**

When a reading is especially difficult, Dickman also hands out **4–5 reading-guide questions**, and each student answers **1–2** of them.

### Group setup

- Form **3–6 groups** of **at least 3 students** each.
- **Rotate group membership about four times a semester.** This makes students build dialogues with classmates they don't already know, not just their friends.
- This goes further than "think, pair, share" because students have to arrive *prepared* for dialogue. Dickman tells students half-jokingly that the assignment proves they did the reading: if he were a student and could skip it, he probably would.

### In-class sequence

```
Round 1: each student shares their WORD      + why it stood out
Round 2: each student shares their PHRASE    + why it stood out
Round 3: each student shares their SENTENCE  + why it stood out
     ↓
Each group prepares 1–2 items to share with the whole class
     ↓
Instructor uses them as prompts for lecture / whole-class discussion
```

The routine helps students capture the essence of a text, or what "speaks" to them. It also prompts dialogue about *why* a given item stands out.

## Why it works: what the protocol protects

NSRF describes its protocols as aids to group dialogue. Each one guards against a common failure:

| The protocol protects… | …instead of |
|---|---|
| Time for **active listening** | Anticipatory interruptions and talking over one another |
| **Equity of voices** | Defaulting to a few dominant speakers |
| A sense of **safety to ask questions** and get feedback | Students feeling attacked or defensive |
| Focus on **specific texts and words** to build understanding | Endless complaining or rehashing the same problems |
| **Enhanced perspective, empowerment, optimism** about next steps | Leaving with no sense of progress |

### Why not the "Four A's"?

Dickman also tried the **"Four A's"** protocol. In it, readers bring something they **A**gree with, want to **A**rgue with, **A**spire to, and an **A**ssumption in the text. He found it left too much room to misunderstand the reading. As he puts it, many of us are "on the ready to argue before understanding."

The "three things" strategy keeps students focused on **understanding first**. It also shows them how hard it really is to understand a complicated text. This idea comes back throughout the book: understanding a claim means reconstructing the question it answers (see Chapters 2, 3, and 8).

### Evidence (anecdotal)

- In end-of-semester evaluations, students regularly say they are satisfied with and appreciate the practice.
- Dickman sees "remarkable improvement" in formative and summative writing.
- He suggests quantitative research could measure these gains.

He recommends using the strategy for **every reading assignment**, whether or not the chapter-specific activities are used.

## How the rest of this repo uses it

Each chapter folder ends with an **Instructor's Suggestions** section taken from the matching part of the appendix. In almost every chapter Dickman says to use the "three things" protocol *in addition to* the chapter activities. As you read each summary, try the strategy yourself:

```markdown
### My three things — <Chapter>

- **Word:** "<word>" (Dickman, p. __) — why it stood out: …
- **Phrase:** "<phrase>" (Dickman, p. __) — why it stood out: …
- **Sentence:** "<sentence>" (Dickman, p. __) — why it stood out: …
```

### Related teaching moves mentioned in the appendix

- **Think, pair, share:** students think alone, discuss with a neighbor, then share with the class. Dickman uses it for most chapter-specific prompts.
- **Reading-guide questions:** 4–5 offered, 1–2 answered, for difficult readings.
- **Rotating groups:** about four rotations a semester.
